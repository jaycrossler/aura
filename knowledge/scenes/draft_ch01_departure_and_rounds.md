# Chapter 1 - Departure and Rounds

> *Most people do not know this, but you can hear a spaceship breathe. A thousand tons of gurgling water move through the walls to balance every footstep. I spent four months falling asleep inside a very large stomach that was politely pretending not to notice me.*
> - Jace Grant, *Falcon* transit logs

<!-- beat:t01_departure_and_rounds.beat1_departure_burn -->
Mars let go of the *Falcon* the way Mars let go of everything: reluctantly, and with paperwork.

Jace felt the departure burn in his teeth. Nuclear drives did not roar through a ship. The movies lied about that, along with most things worth doing. The thrust pressed instead, a firm hand in the small of his back that did not get tired.

He lay webbed into an acceleration couch in the number-three common module with a fifteen-pound brindle puppy strapped to his chest. Jace had printed the harness himself after Origin Industries classified Cerberus as cargo, and cargo did not receive acceleration couches.

"You are not cargo," he told the dog.

Cerberus answered by trying to burrow through his sternum.

Through the module's single viewport, Terminus dwindled beneath them. The domes caught the sun first. Then the rail lines vanished into the red. Somewhere below waited a base that had thrown him out, a family of puppies being divided among buyers, and a debt with more digits than his service number.

Mars shrank from a world to a coin.

The pressure eased near the end of the first watch. Cerberus stopped trembling, considered the harness straps, and began chewing through the one part that kept him alive.

"You may need to rethink your priorities."

Cerberus wagged without stopping.

Water whispered through the walls. It moved in long, soft surges around the rotating ring, never quite stopping. Jace had heard the pumps during loading, but under thrust they became the ship's pulse. Ballast shifted to counter people, livestock, and cargo moving through the ring. The same water put mass between them and the radiation outside.

Six pigs changed sides in their berth. A pump spun up somewhere aft.

The *Falcon* disagreed with the pigs and moved several hundred liters the other way.

Jace smiled despite himself.

"That will stop being funny," Maureen said from the couch across the aisle.

The captain did not look at him. She watched load figures crawl over a tablet fixed to her forearm. Four crew, fifty-four animals, thirty-six berths, and enough cargo to make staying on Mars impossible all pulled at her attention.

The passenger column showed zero. The entire Chinese mining crew had withdrawn at the last minute. No settlers had replaced them. The cargo contracts had not withdrawn with the miners, so the *Falcon* had launched heavy and nearly empty, with Maureen paying to keep unused berths cold while her passage revenue sat on Mars.

Origin had hired Jace in seventy-two hours because Mei needed a backup animal tender and Maureen needed another pair of hands. Maureen had accepted him with the warmth normally reserved for an emergency patch.

"The pigs?"

"The alarms." She pointed at a screen in the wall with flashing yellow alerts. Now that Jace looked, there were many scattered on every wall. "The pigs will remain funny."

Maureen glanced at him once, weighing whether a reply would encourage him. She returned to the tablet.

That was his welcome aboard.

Near the end of the fourth month, *Falcon's* ring had become his world.

Metis Station was a week ahead, though not along their path. Fortuna was another two weeks beyond it, close enough now that ordinary conversation no longer required messages composed for a person who had already gone to bed.

Jace learned *Falcon's* mornings by smell. Coffee and disinfectant near the galley. Hot polymer near the forge. Warm hay, wet feathers, and the steady evidence of mammalian digestion along the livestock spine. The ballast water followed him behind the walls, compensating for each footstep like a patient accountant.

The headache arrived before the lights did.

He lay still and took inventory. Pressure behind both eyes: present. Heat around the implant at his left temple: present. Breathing mask sealed over his nose and mouth: present, because he had read the decompression statistics and refused to become one.

Cerberus had rotated during the night until all four paws pointed at the wall. Low gravity let him sleep like an overturned table.

Jace peeled off the sleep breather mask, dry-swallowed his radiation tablet and the anti-nausea pill that kept it company, and gave the pain sixty seconds to declare its intentions. He pulled off Cerberus's sleep mask as well - specially printed with aerogel to fit the extended puppy snout which somehow was able to still snore.

The pain faded to a warm burning coal roasting behind his left eye.

Dr. Lanchee's diagnostics had found nothing. The implant ran warm overnight. His body was adapting to another gravity change. All ordinary explanations, neatly filed. The dreams were less neat: crystal corridors, transparent walls, angles that continued after he looked away. They dissolved each morning before he could describe them.

He had stopped trying.

A message from Patrick waited beside yesterday's repair report. A video feed from one of the dozen ship robots zoomed in on the weld he'd made.

*Good fix on panel R7-04. Corner anchor is a little hot, but it should hold. Give it a good kick today to see that it's strong.*

Far sunward, the ship's co-owner and Maureen's husband tended sealant by mail.

Jace tagged the panel for inspection and swung his feet to the deck.

"Up, boy. Rounds."

Cerberus transformed from furniture into dog in less than a second.

The waste station in the washroom had been used... mostly. A substantial deposit rested beside the grate with the confident placement of something that had aimed and then declined.

Jace stared at it. Cerberus stared at him.

"Strong opening argument. Poor follow-through."

He cleaned the floor, photographed the narrow grate, and sent a maintenance request. He tagged it to Patrick, instead of their intrepid Captain.

*Washroom needs a second fan for better circulation at foot-level. I've added a quieter replacement to the maintenance queue. Can you approve?*

He attached the airflow readings. He did not attach the photograph. If Jace ordered the fan himself, Maureen would ask why. Patrick could make it look like remote preventive maintenance, provided Patrick agreed that some engineering facts did not improve with wider distribution.

Mei stood in the galley with one hand around a mug and the other feeding a scrap of egg to Siren. Cerberus saw his sister and launched himself. Siren stepped aside without looking. He hit the cabinet, rebounded in four-tenths gravity, and landed facing the wrong direction.

The coffee machine debited five Prime tokens for coffee, sugar, and something labeled *cream analogue*. Jace watched a gray ribbon curl into his cup.

"Five tokens?"

"Cancelled passengers," Maureen said from the table. "Lost fares. Consumables still cost money."

Jace opened the machine's public price history in his HUD. "But these prices haven't changed in seven years. I use all but one of my Prime tokens just to buy food and drink daily."

Maureen scowled.

The coffee machine blipped. "New price update!"

Coffee now cost one token more.

Mei glared at Jace over the rim of her mug.

Jace took a careful sip. "I have improved the voyage economy."

Mei watched him recover. She ran the livestock operation. Jace carried tools, handled maintenance, and served as the backup Origin had hired for this one crossing.

"He will learn," Jace said.

"Maybe."

Siren stole the rest of the egg.

It tied with the most words she had spoke to him at breakfast in a month.

They took both dogs down the livestock spine. Jace clipped Cerberus to the outer rail before the sheep module. The puppy leaned forward until the tether held him horizontal, every part of him convinced that twelve lambs needed immediate organization.

"No entering. No licking. No career decisions."

Cerberus looked up with grave attention.

"Good. You understood none of that."

Inside, one lamb startled when another lamb's ear brushed her cheek. She jumped sideways, hung in the low gravity long enough to reconsider her choices, and landed among eleven animals who had accepted her panic without requesting evidence.

For thirty seconds, the enclosure became weather.

Jace waited for the wool to settle. "Your ancestors survived wolves. You have been defeated by an ear."

As he fed and watered and scrubbed poop off the floor, his HUD pinged with a high priority email. It took a few blinks, but he finally opened it. From Mei, it just read *Subject: did you eat my duck?*

Jace paused. What did that mean? He squinted and scrunched his face, eventually pulling up her location on the ship map.  She was in the insect container, feeding and cleaning them.  Jace hated feeding the vats of insects, though he did like the protein powder you could blend them into.  He had no idea what she meant.

The geese guarded their hatch in a line, eight necks raised with the posture of customs officers who had found an undeclared sandwich. Cerberus moved behind Jace's leg, back straight and regal. The puppy had learned that Geese bit anyone they felt they could dominate. Hard.

"Correct," Jace told him. "Respect jurisdiction."

Jace serviced their water from the side port while the lead goose struck the scanner twice and won both encounters.

The ducks had defeated Jace's splash guard. Water covered the textured floor, though the pan held the exact amount it had held yesterday. Eleven female birds moved through the enclosure with great purpose and no destination.

Jace counted them again.

"Eleven."

"Manifest says twelve."

He checked the access panel. Sealed. The ventilation screen. Intact. Waste line. Too narrow, which he verified against the ducks and regretted immediately. There was no place on a rotating ship for a duck to go. Ducks did not sublimate.

Cerberus pressed his nose to the observation strip. Eleven ducks pressed closer to inspect him.

"You move one?" Jace asked.

Cerberus gave him a look that reduced his available follow-up questions to zero.

He looked up Mei's last text.  She sent the message after breakfast. Writing gave her more room than spoken English.

Jace wrote back from the pig module while Screwdriver worried at a service-panel latch with the focus of a doctoral candidate.

*I did not eat your duck. I inspected every duck-egress route. There are no duck-egress routes. Your duck has left a sealed vessel in interplanetary space, and I would like to register that this bothers me.*

Mei's reply arrived while he added a clamp to the latch the pigs had found a way to open. We waved to the cameras, knowing the pigs were more popular across the solar system than he was.

*Me too.*

Screwdriver watched his hands, then tested the clamp with his mouth before Jace finished tightening it.

"Spit out the spacecraft," Jace said.

The pig dropped a strip of gasket material, turned around, then pooped on it.

Cerberus sat outside the module, vibrating with admiration.

"You may not apprentice with the pigs."

Artemis called that evening.

Her face appeared above his workbench, live from Metis Station. No send queue. No stale timestamp. For the first time in five years, Jace could see his sister react to him in real time instead of receiving a polished answer to something he had said hours or days before.

"Happy birthday, little brother." Her crooked smile faltered and returned. "I know what today was supposed to look like. Captain's bars. Mom crying on the comm. All of it. I am sorry it looks like this instead."

She glanced off-camera. Jace saw it happen and answered before the moment escaped.

"What was that?"

"Work."

"That was the Artemis version of work, which means either classified or on fire."

"Only busy. Good busy. Mostly." Her reply came immediately. The absence of delay felt like a fifth person in the room.

"It's so weird you're closer now. Weeks away instead of years. Things here are busy. Good busy. Mostly." Another glance, too quick to read. "Eat something that was alive this morning. That is an order. I outrank you now. I have a desk."

"I am all right," Jace said. "Cerberus is not foodstock."

"I can hear him chewing your bench."

Jace looked down. Cerberus stopped attacking the faux-wood with his teeth and attempted a wide-eyed innocence.

When the call ended, Jace stayed in the workshop because his bench there felt farther from everyone than the Belt did.

--

Nick's latest challenge waited in their arena. Jace accepted through his HUD and lost in eighty-three seconds.

The military had once tuned the implant within his skull until intention became command. He had commanded thirty drones without thinking about menus, gesture layers, or which eye held focus. After discharge, the service had wiped those trained mappings and returned the civilian partition to factory standard. The hardware remained in his skull. The practiced interface did not. Some of the others that had been kicked out related it to relearning to walk after a coma.

Now three simulated mechs crowded his attention. Two he could manage. A third turned every command into a negotiation with his own visual cortex. Nick won while Jace was still trying to select the correct set of attack commands.

*You are getting better,* Nick sent.

*You won every game.*

*Both statements can be true.*

*Slower. Also, I saved a mech for you. It is short. You will understand why this is funny when we meet.*

Jace started another match. Nick won that one too, but Jace kept three units moving for thirty seconds before one walked into a wall. Yesterday it had been twenty.

Cerberus slept across his boots. The *Falcon* gurgled water through the walls.

Two weeks to Fortuna. Two million Tokens of debt. A missing duck. A friend he'd never met saving him a machine shaped like a joke.

This birthday should have felt worse than it did.

Behind his left eye, the burning coal flared with pain.
