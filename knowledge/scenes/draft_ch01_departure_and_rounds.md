# Chapter 1. Departure and Rounds

> *Apollo: I cannot decide whether scrubbing chicken splatters is worse than the stink-bombs the geese make. They are little balls of fury, and somehow their waste retains that emotion.*
>
> *Artemis: What about the puppies? Potty trained yet?*
>
> *Athena: You should have brought a cat. Cats would be better on starships.*
>
> *Artemis: No. Remember your kitten? It peed everywhere. Cat urine inside a ship would get into the cracks, short something out, and cause a murder.*
>
> *Apollo: One cleaning robot got dog poop on its wheel and pressed it into the carpet. Maureen was furious.*

Mars let go of the *Falcon* the way Mars let go of everything: reluctantly, and with paperwork.

Jace felt the departure burn in his teeth. Nuclear drives did not roar through a ship. The movies lied about that, along with most things worth doing. The thrust pressed instead, a firm hand in the small of his back that did not get tired.

He lay webbed into an acceleration couch in the common module with a fifteen-pound puppy strapped to his chest. Jace had printed the harness himself after Origin Industries classified Cerberus as cargo, and cargo did not receive acceleration couches.

"You are not cargo," he told the dog.

Cerberus answered by trying to burrow through his sternum.

Through the module's single viewport, Terminus dwindled beneath them. The domes caught the sun first. Then the rail lines vanished into the rusted terrain. Somewhere below waited a base that the newly elected government had thrown him out of and a debt with more digits than his service number. The dogs loved zero gravity and being able to run on the ceiling, but cleaning up floating dog pee was miserable for everyone involved. The other animals in transit hated it, and he didn't look forward to getting them cleaned up. Jace missed gravity, even the low gravity that the *Falcon* was spinning up to.

Mars shrank from a world to a coin.

The pressure eased near the end of the first day. The spin was up to Mars standard, a third of the Earth gravity Jace had been born with. Cerberus stopped trembling, considered the harness straps, and began chewing through the one part that kept him alive.

"You may need to rethink your priorities."

Cerberus wagged without stopping.

Water whispered through the walls. It moved in long, soft surges around the rotating ring, never quite stopping. Jace had heard the pumps during loading, but under thrust they became the ship's pulse. Ballast shifted to counter people, livestock, and cargo moving through the ring. The same water put mass between them and the radiation outside.

Six pigs changed sides in their berth. A pump spun up somewhere aft.

The *Falcon* disagreed with the pigs and moved several hundred liters the other way.

Jace smiled despite himself.

"That will stop being funny," Maureen said from the couch across the aisle.

"The pigs?" he asked.

"The alarms will." She pointed at a screen in the wall with flashing yellow alerts. Now that Jace looked, there were screens embedded in every wall. "The pigs will remain funny."

The captain did not look at him. She watched load figures crawl over a small screen fixed to her forearm. Four crew, fifty-four animals, thirty-six berths, dozens of maintenance drones, and enough cargo to make staying on Mars impossible all pulled at her attention.

The passenger column showed zero. An entire Chinese mining crew had withdrawn at the last minute. No settlers had replaced them. The cargo contracts had not withdrawn with the miners, so the *Falcon* had launched heavy and nearly empty, with Maureen paying to keep unused berths cold while her passage revenue sat on Mars.

"They were already aboard," Maureen said. "Spent a full day inspecting the engine spaces and the central spire with their charter engineer. Signed the access log. Asked sensible questions. Then all of them cancelled." She tapped the column of numbers like she was flicking something off her sleeve. 

"I had three days to fill a manifest built for passengers, and Origin's launch window doesn't move for anybody's convenience but its own. So I bought what I could get. Feed supplement. Actual soil. Who ships dirt? Me, I guess. God, I miss Patrick. Instead I'm hauling ballast at the least profitable margin available to me, because Origin would rather I launch a heavy, cheap ship on schedule than launch a light, valuable one two weeks late."

Origin had hired Jace in those seventy-two hours because Mei needed a backup animal tender and Maureen needed another pair of hands. Maureen had accepted him with the warmth normally reserved for a colonoscopy.

She had been a Navy chief before she met Patrick while he was working on military ships. She left the service, and together they bought the old frame they rebuilt into the *Falcon*. Jace had known a few Navy people and Maureen had the same compressed annoyed authority: every sentence sounded as if she was expecting an argument.

"Well, I hope I can be of use."

Maureen glanced at him once, weighing whether a reply would encourage him. She returned to typing on her wrist.

That was his welcome aboard.

Three weeks in, Jace started running before the ship's lights came up, because Lanchee's diagnostics kept finding nothing wrong with him and he had decided to make that true by force.

He wore a weighted vest printed from the same polymer stock as everything else on the *Falcon*. It carried eighty pounds distributed evenly enough not to fight his spine as he ran through the ship's ring. The long, curved hallway was basically a tube with a ship built around it. Within the corridor, maintenance drones outnumbered the people. Weight at Mars-standard gravity was much less, but momentum made it much heavier the faster you moved. Jace was learning to jump and keep his torso following the same path to avoid turning the vest into an 80-pound bear hug.

Cerberus ran beside him, bouncing and loping along the old worn carpet. Siren, who normally treated Jace with the polite indifference she extended to furniture, had taken to falling into step a length behind Cerberus's shoulder, which Jace had come to understand was not affection for her brother so much as an unwillingness to let the puppy have an experience she wasn't personally supervising.

"You're 1 hour older than him. Get over yourself." Siren didn't even look at Jace in response.

The corridor made a full circuit of the ring in about three minutes at a slow jog, which meant Jace spent his mornings watching the same 36 doors go by from a slightly different angle each lap, like a record with a scratch in it. Animal pods. Clinic. Wash room. His room. Mei's room. Galley. Cargo pods. Maureen's cabin. Forge. Common module. Observatory. Empty cabins. And repeat. And repeat.

The *Falcon* was a spinning top pointed at their target. You could feel it rather than see it. The ring itself was the rim of a wheel, with three long spokes running inward to the spire where Patrick's nuclear drive did the actual work of moving them through space. The whole assembly spun to fake the gravity that let pigs stand up and coffee stay in its cup. When you walked the ring you were walking with your feet towards the outside of the circle, so "up" was always towards the axis shaft in the middle. The faster it spun, the more gravity. And the more nausea passengers and animals felt.

Bolted and welded to the outside of the ring, wherever there was structural purchase to take the load, hung the berths and cargo canisters: 36 rectangular shipping-container modules the size of a small apartment, standardized to fit an ocean-going cargo ship from Earth or a Mars train, and to slot into the same mounting rails whether they carried spare panel stock or a person's entire life. Lanchee's clinic was one of those, dark and quiet at this hour, her exam table and her medical stores and a cozy apartment with a luxurious bed he'd glimpsed once all riding along on the outside of a ring nobody had designed with a doctor's office in mind.

The forge and machine shop occupied two canisters connected end to end. Together, they made a surprisingly long workspace, as long as the swim-team pool Jace had used growing up. It ran hot before the ship's day officially began and carried extra shielding in case anything exploded during production. Jace ran past both every few minutes, the vest making his shoulders ache in a way that felt, mercifully, like nothing except effort. Most cargo doors and empty berths were permanently closed this trip, so he was getting sick of seeing the same few doors.

Jace had been athletic and fit while in college and then in the military. The Air Force had made sure of it, and he intended to still be fit on the far side of a five-year Fortuna contract that started, technically, the moment this crossing ended.

Robots did not make the run easy. A maintenance drone hauling a spool of cable had exactly no interest in Jace's training schedule and occupied the center of the corridor with the confidence of a cart that had right of way. He hopped over it. Two laps later, a different drone had wedged itself sideways across the passage to clean an air filter, and Jace ran the tight arc around it with Cerberus practically vertical on the curved wall, delighted.

"You're getting good at that," he told the dog, not slowing.

Cerberus's answering bark cost him his footing entirely, and for one lap he ran on three legs and general enthusiasm.

Behind them both, unbothered, Siren kept pace like she'd been doing it her whole life, which, Jace supposed, counting up her age against their time aboard, she more or less had.

Near the end of the fourth month, *Falcon's* ring had become his world.

Adama Station lay off their forward quarter, though not along their path. Fortuna was seventeen days ahead, close enough now that ordinary conversation no longer required messages composed for a person who had already gone to bed.

Jace learned *Falcon's* mornings by smell. Coffee and disinfectant near the galley. Hot polymer near the forge. Warm hay, wet feathers, and the steady evidence of mammalian digestion in the livestock pods. The ballast water followed him behind the walls, compensating for each footstep like a patient accountant.

The headache arrived before the lights did.

He lay still and took inventory. Pressure behind both eyes: present. Heat around the implant at his left temple: present. Breathing mask sealed over his nose and mouth: present, because he had read the decompression statistics and refused to become one.

Cerberus had rotated during the night until all four paws pointed at the wall. Low gravity let him sleep like an overturned table.

Jace peeled off the sleep breather mask, dry-swallowed his radiation tablet, the anti-nausea pill that kept it company, and the small chalky third pill the base flight surgeon had put him on years ago for the bone condition that had, in the end, been a footnote to the reason he'd actually been discharged, but a footnote he still had to take every morning regardless, low gravity or not, and gave the pain sixty seconds to declare its intentions. He pulled off Cerberus's sleep mask as well, specially printed with aerogel to fit the extended puppy snout which somehow was able to still snore despite air blowing into it.

The pain faded to a warm burning coal roasting behind his left eye.

Dr. Lanchee's diagnostics had found nothing. The implant ran warm overnight. His body was adapting to another gravity change. All ordinary explanations, neatly filed. The dreams were less neat: crystal corridors, transparent walls, angles that continued after he looked away. They dissolved each morning before he could describe them.

He had stopped trying.

A message from Patrick waited beside yesterday's repair report. A video feed from one of the dozen ship robots zoomed in on the weld he'd made.

*Good fix on panel R7-04. Corner anchor is a little hot, but it should hold. Give it a good kick today to see that it's strong.*

Far sunward, the ship's co-owner and Maureen's husband tended sealant from a hospital bed back on Mars.

Jace tagged the panel for inspection and swung his feet to the deck.

"Up, boy. Rounds."

Cerberus transformed from furniture into dog in less than a second.

The waste pad in the washroom had been used... mostly. A substantial deposit rested beside the grate with the confident placement of something that had aimed and then declined. The smell was overpowering. Maureen would be furious if she'd seen it. She was very vocal about not liking dogs on her ship.

Jace stared at it. Cerberus stared at him.

"Your aim is not improving."

He cleaned the floor, photographed the narrow grate using his HUD, and sent a maintenance request. He tagged it to Patrick instead of their intrepid captain.

*Washroom needs a second fan for better circulation at dog-level. I've added a quieter replacement to the maintenance queue. Can you approve?*

He attached the airflow readings and the photograph. If Jace ordered the fan himself, Maureen would ask why. Patrick could make it look like remote preventive maintenance, provided Patrick agreed that some engineering facts did not improve with wider distribution.

Mei stood in the galley with one hand around a mug and the other feeding a scrap of egg to Siren. Cerberus saw his sister and launched himself. Siren stepped aside without looking. Cerberus sailed past, overbalanced and delighted with himself, eyes widening too late to do anything about it. She pivoted and tackled him down into the deck plating with the flat, patient competence of a sumo wrestler winning an argument. Cerberus lay there a moment, pinned, tail waggling, entirely satisfied with the outcome despite having lost decisively. Siren gnawed his ear, sprang off him, retrieved the rest of the egg from where it had fallen, and returned to Mei's side as if nothing had happened.

The coffee machine debited his account five tokens for coffee, sugar, and something labeled *cream analogue*. Jace watched a gray ribbon curl into his cup.

"I should have brought this up before, but why is coffee five tokens?"

"Cancelled passengers," Maureen said from the table. "Lost fares. Consumables still cost money."

Jace opened the machine's public price history in his HUD. "But these prices haven't changed in seven years. I use all but one of my Prime tokens just to buy food and two coffees daily."

Maureen scowled, then walked out of the galley.

The coffee machine blipped. "New price update!"

Coffee now cost six tokens.

Mei glared at Jace over the rim of her mug.

Jace took a careful sip, still panting. "Next time, stop me from complaining."

Mei watched him regain his breath after the run. She ran the livestock operation. Jace carried tools, handled maintenance, and served as the backup Origin had hired for this one crossing. A contract that ended, on paper, the day the *Falcon* docked, and began again immediately after in a different shape: five years on Fortuna Station itself, which was the only arithmetic anyone had offered him that actually closed out the debt before he was an old man.

Mei held out another piece of egg. The first chicks had hatched last week, and they were still celebrating. Cerberus jumped for it, but missed and wound up slamming his head into the counter, yelping.

"He will learn," Jace said.

"Maybe."

It tied with the most words she had spoken to him in a month.

Siren stole the rest of the egg.

There were fifty-eight women currently aboard the *Falcon*. You'd think most men would love those odds. Maureen, Mei, and Lanchee among the humans. Fifty-four head of breeding stock and Siren among the animals. Every last one of them female, because Origin didn't ship a livestock founder population any other way; you built a herd from does and sows and ewes, and you bought your genetics separately, frozen, in a canister the size of a lunchbox. Which left exactly two males within the hull: Jace, and a fifteen-pound dog currently attempting to relitigate his loss to Siren by chewing on her tail.

"We're woefully outnumbered," Jace told him. "Read the room."

Cerberus did not read the room. He rarely did.

They took both dogs down to the livestock pods for his cleaning shift. Jace had grown up on a horse farm in Virginia. A pod was like a miniature barn, built to a standard size for cargo ships traveling from Earth or Mars to the belt. Each was self-contained, with its own air, humidity, and water systems to prevent contamination or disease from spreading. Jace clipped Cerberus to the outer rail before the sheep module. The puppy leaned forward until the tether held him horizontal, every part of him convinced that twelve lambs needed immediate organization.

"No entering. No licking. No herding."

Cerberus looked up with grave attention.

"Good. You understood none of that."

Inside, one lamb startled when another lamb's head brushed her cheek. She jumped sideways, hung in the low gravity long enough to reconsider her choices, and landed among eleven animals who had accepted her panic without requesting evidence.

For thirty seconds, the enclosure became weather.

Jace waited for the cloud of wool to settle. "Your ancestors survived wolves. You have been defeated by an ear."

As he fed and watered and scrubbed poop off the floor, his HUD pinged with a high-priority message. It took a few blinks, but he finally opened it. From Mei, it just read *Subject: did you eat my duck?*

Jace paused. What did that mean? He squinted and scrunched his face, eventually pulling up her location on the ship map. She was in the insect container, feeding and cleaning them. Jace hated feeding the vats of insects, though he did like the protein powder you could blend them into. He had no idea what she meant.

The geese guarded their hatch in a line, eight necks raised with the posture of customs officers who had found an undeclared sandwich. Cerberus moved behind Jace's leg, back straight and regal. The puppy had learned that geese bit anyone they felt they could dominate. Hard.

"Correct," Jace told him. "Respect jurisdiction."

Jace serviced their water from the side port while the lead goose struck the scanner twice and won both encounters.

He moved into the duck enclosure, sweating in the much higher humidity. The fowl had destroyed the splash guard Jace had printed. Water covered the textured floor, but their bowl was still full. Mei must have been here. Eleven female birds moved through the enclosure with great purpose and no destination.

Jace counted them again.

"Eleven. Manifest says twelve."

He checked the access panel. Sealed. The ventilation screen. Intact. Waste line. Too narrow, which he verified against the ducks and regretted immediately. There was no place on a rotating ship for a duck to go. Ducks did not sublimate.

Cerberus pressed his nose to the observation pane. Eleven ducks pressed closer to inspect him.

"You mess with one?" Jace asked.

Cerberus gave him a look that reduced his available follow-up questions to zero.

He looked up Mei's last text. She sent the message after breakfast. Writing gave her more room than spoken English.

Jace wrote back from the pig module while Screwdriver worried at a service panel with the focus of a doctoral candidate.

*I did not eat your duck. I inspected every duck-egress route. There are no duck-egress routes. Your duck has left a sealed vessel in interplanetary space, and I would like to register that this bothers me.*

Mei's reply arrived while he added a clamp to the latch the pigs had found a way to open. He waved to the cameras, knowing the pigs were more popular across the solar system than he was.

*Me too. The silkworms are also not breeding well this week. 2 liters less than predicted.*

*I guess they didn't eat your duck either.*

Screwdriver watched his hands, then tested the clamp with her mouth before Jace finished tightening it.

"Spit out the spacecraft," Jace said.

The pig dropped a strip of gasket material, turned around, then defecated on it.

Cerberus sat outside the module, vibrating with admiration.

"You may not apprentice with the pigs."

Artemis called that evening.

Her face appeared above his workbench, live from Adama Station. No send queue. No stale timestamp. For the first time in five years, Jace could see his sister react to him in real time instead of receiving a polished answer to something he had said hours or days before.

"Happy birthday, little brother." Her crooked smile faltered and returned. "I know what today was supposed to look like. Captain's bars. Mom crying on the comm. All of it. I am sorry it looks like this instead."

She glanced off-camera. Jace saw it happen and answered before the moment escaped.

"What was that?"

"Work."

"That was the Artemis version of work, which means either classified or on fire."

"Only busy. Good busy. Mostly." Her reply came immediately. The absence of delay felt like a fifth person in the room.

"It's so weird you're closer now. Weeks away instead of years. Things here are busy. Good busy. Mostly." Another glance, too quick to read. "Eat something that was alive this morning. That is an order. I outrank you now. I have a desk."

"I am all right," Jace said. "Cerberus is not foodstock."

"I can hear him chewing your bench."

Jace looked down. Cerberus stopped attacking the faux-wood with his teeth and attempted a wide-eyed innocence.

When the call ended, Jace stayed in the workshop because his bench there felt farther from everyone than the Belt did.

##

Nick's latest challenge waited in their arena. Jace accepted through his HUD and lost in eighty-three seconds.

They'd met months back the same way most of the belt met, online. A public strategy-game lobby, Jace killing time between HUD retraining drills, Nick already four moves into a build order that made no sense until it did. That first match had taken most of an evening, half of it spent typing rather than playing, because the light-lag between Mars and wherever Nick had been at the time made anything faster than a slow-turn strategy game a lesson in patience. Four months and a shrinking gap later, they'd played enough matches, at short enough intervals, that the games had started to feel less like turns exchanged across the solar system and closer to two people in the same room, which Jace understood was mostly an illusion built out of narrowing seconds, and which he had stopped correcting himself about.

The military had once tuned the implant within his skull until intention became command. He had commanded thirty drones without thinking about menus, gesture layers, or which eye held focus. After discharge, the service had wiped those trained mappings and returned the civilian partition to factory standard. The hardware remained in his skull. The practiced interface did not. Others who had been kicked out compared it to relearning to walk after a coma.

*What does it feel like?* Nick sent.

Jace read the message twice. *You do not have a HUD?*

*I have overlay glasses. They cost less than a house. An implant leaves you blind for a month while your brain recovers.*

Jace had assumed Nick was implanted. Everyone who beat him this badly should have needed surgery.

He tried to explain. Sending a command felt like controlling exactly one tooth nerve. Not his jaw. Not his tongue. One nerve under one tooth, pulsed in precisely the right way. If he hit the neighboring pathway, the HUD switched to infrared and opened an email folder while his mech walked into enemy fire.

Eight cameras in his eyeballs and brow triangulated the room around him. Before the military wipe, his implant had fused their feeds without effort. Now, if he tried to watch anything through all eight cameras in full three dimensions, a migraine began behind his left eye.

*That sounds terrible,* Nick sent.

*It used to feel better.*

Now three simulated mechs crowded his attention. Two he could manage. A third turned every command into a negotiation with his own visual cortex. Nick won while Jace was still trying to select the correct set of attack commands.

*You are getting better,* Nick sent.

*You won every game.*

*Both statements can be true.*

*Slower. Also, I saved a mech for you. It is short. You will understand why this is funny when we meet.*

Jace considered that for a second. Belters usually ran tall. Low gravity, growth plates that never got the memo to stop, and Nick had been beating him at command-queue mecha battles for four months with the unhurried confidence of someone looking down at the board from a height. Jace pictured him accordingly: rangy, stretched, the kind of tall that ducked through doorways out of habit. It would be funny, Jace decided, if the short mech turned out to be some kind of height joke. He filed the theory away, pleased with it, and was wrong about every part of it, though he wouldn't learn that for another two weeks.

Jace started another match. Nick won that one too, but Jace kept three units moving for thirty seconds before one walked into a wall. Yesterday it had been twenty.

Cerberus slept across his boots. The *Falcon* gurgled water through the walls.

Seventeen days to Fortuna. Two million Tokens of debt. A missing duck. A friend he'd never met saving him a machine shaped like a joke.

This birthday should have felt worse than it did.

Behind his left eye, the burning coal flared with migraine-inducing pain. OK, now it felt worse.
