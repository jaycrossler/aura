# Chapter 26. Division of Labor

> *"A photon is not secretly a wave or secretly a particle; it is both, and which one you get depends on what you ask it. I am beginning to suspect the Astral works the same way, and that we simply have not found the right question yet."*
> Sophia Lotte, working notebook

##

Jace caught himself doing it again on a Tuesday. Standing in the doorway of Sophia's room, which had stopped being Sophia's room in any meaningful sense weeks ago and become something closer to a field station, watching Nikos argue with Brandon about propellant ratios, half his attention already halfway across the station toward Drone Ops, where he was also, technically, supposed to be a person with a job.

"You're doing the thing," Sophia said, not looking up from her notebook.

"What thing."

"The thing where you try to personally verify every piece of everything, as if the rest of us might quietly combust if you look away."

"Brandon works with things that quietly combust."

"That is not the same thing and you know it."

He did know it. He'd known it for about a week, in the specific way you know something is true right before you're ready to admit it changes anything. Origin's contract didn't stop needing him. Drone Ops didn't stop needing him. Cerberus didn't stop needing walks. And now there was a rocket, and a lab, and a growing list of people who each needed a piece of his attention that he did not currently have enough of to give.

"Nikos knows more about propulsion chemistry than I do," he said, mostly to himself.

"He does."

"Brandon knows more about explosives than either of us."

"He does that too."

"So what exactly do they need me for."

Sophia finally looked up. "To sign off on the parts that would kill someone if they got it wrong, and otherwise to get out of the way."

It was not a comfortable thing to hear. It was also, Jace suspected, the first genuinely useful piece of leadership advice anyone had given him since a training officer eight years and one whole life ago had told him that a good commander's job was mostly to make sure other people didn't need him.

"Fine," he said. "Fine. New rule. Everybody owns their own piece. I show up when something needs my hands or my signature, and otherwise I stay out of it."

"That was almost graceful."

"Don't get used to it."

##

The rocket, when it finally stood upright on its improvised gantry in Ring Three's least-used bay, looked less like a spaceship and more like something Origin had already written off twice and Nikos had personally resurrected out of spite. Which was, roughly, accurate. Brandon had rebuilt the fuel lines himself, testing each joint with a patience that made Jace's own hands itch to help and made him, per the new rule, stay by the console instead.

"You're allowed to touch things," Nikos said, not unkindly, watching Jace hover.

"I'm supervising."

"You are hovering. There's a difference and everyone can see it."

Jace laughed despite himself and made himself useful the way he was actually useful, running the launch sequence checks, cross-referencing {Aura}'s structural read against Brandon's numbers, being the second set of eyes rather than the only set. He crossed briefly to confirm the Astral-side anchor point Sophia had marked, came back, signed off, and let Nikos push the button.

The launch itself was small, contained, and genuinely thrilling in the way that only something built mostly by hand ever is. It cleared the bay, stabilized, and began its slow, patient climb toward the orbit Sparky would use as a physical-space reference point.

Nikos whooped. Brandon allowed himself something close to a smile.

"Two Fortunas cared enough to grow food in a can," Nikos said, apropos of nothing, watching the readout climb. "One cares enough to build a rocket out of spite. I like this station."

Later, walking back through Ring Two, Jace passed the outer plots along the Barn's edge, chicken wire dividers, hand lettered names gone soft with humidity. Fatima Otieno's coriander had bolted again. Diego Reyes Almeida's chili peppers glowed faintly red under the grow lights, absurd and thriving. Someone's marigolds had annexed two plots over without objection.

He had never planted anything. It occurred to him, watching a woman he didn't know water a row of something green and hopeful, that he might want to.

##

Hayes wasn't at the table.

Jace noticed on the second morning and told himself it meant nothing. Hayes disappeared sometimes, a day here or there, an old friend on Ring One, a Long Burn night that ran too long. But by the third morning Cerberus had started sitting in front of the empty chair out of something that looked less like habit and more like worry, and Jace had started noticing that he noticed.

He asked Erin. She hadn't seen him. He asked the noodle counter, the Barn, Ring One's biscuit vendor, who knew Hayes's order well enough to remark that it hadn't been filled in four days.

He knocked on Hayes's door between one task and the next, mostly to prove himself wrong.

No answer.

He knocked harder. Still nothing. Something in his chest went cold and specific.

Sophia found him standing there with his hand flat against the door.

"Cross with me," he said. "Now."

They didn't wait for authorization.

Hayes lay on his bunk, one arm thrown off the edge, unmoving, his color bad, his breathing shallow and too even, the rhythm of a body running on the smallest program it could manage. Sophia checked him the way they'd learned to check anything now, for the dark folded shape, the pulsing tendrils.

Nothing.

"No tick," she said. "Nothing feeding."

That was worse, somehow. A tick was a problem they understood.

Jace crossed fully and found the wrongness without needing confirmation. Hayes's left arm didn't read right in the Astral. The light of him should have run down in one continuous line and instead folded back on itself partway, doubled and knotted, like something had briefly existed where inside and outside meant something different and then snapped back wrong.

He hadn't been attacked. He'd done this to himself, alone, however he'd been trying to do it.

They got him to the clinic through the service corridor rather than wait for an escort. Cerberus paced beside them the whole way, silent, present.

Lanchee met them at the door already reading vitals off Jace's shared feed. Ten minutes later she delivered her verdict with the particular quiet she used when choosing not to say the other words available to her.

"Traumatic injury. Left arm. I don't have a mechanism. I have a shape that shouldn't exist without a fracture, and there is no fracture."

"He's not going to lose it?" Jace asked.

"He already has, functionally. Whether it stays attached is a different question." She looked at him for a beat too long. "Whatever the two of you have been doing. This is not new to me anymore, is it."

He didn't answer, which was an answer.

She stayed with Hayes past the end of her shift, past the point where staying was required by anything except her own decision, and when she finally sat down across from Sophia the next morning, tired in a way sleep hadn't touched, she said, "Tell me what you need from me. All of it. I'm in."

Nobody cheered. It didn't feel like the kind of thing you cheered.

##

The tick they'd killed clearing the corridor near Hayes's room left an orb nobody wanted to touch. Small, amber gold, pulsing faintly on the deck plating like something that hadn't finished deciding whether it was alive.

"We let these go," Sophia said, mostly to herself, staring at it. "That's the rule we made."

"We did," Nick agreed.

Nobody moved to leave it.

"I want to keep this one," Sophia said finally. "Just this one. We know almost nothing about what these actually are, and we have a room now where we can study something like this properly, away from anyone who could absorb it by accident."

She didn't touch it with her hands. A directed pull of Will lifted it, small and careful, and set it down on a high shelf in the Astral lab, well clear of the doorway, well clear of anywhere a person might casually stand.

"Just this one," she said again, like saying it twice made it a smaller rule to break.

##

Brandon's explosion, when it came two days later, was small as these things went. A propellant mix that ran hotter than predicted, a containment vessel that did its job well enough that nobody was hurt, and a shockwave through the lab's thin air that knocked three things off the shelf, one of which was the orb.

"Sorry," Brandon said, already reaching to pick everything back up before he'd finished coughing the smoke out of his lungs.

He had the orb in his bare hand before anyone thought to stop him.

He went very still.

"Brandon?"

"I'm fine," he said, not sounding fine. "I feel a little sick." He set the orb carefully back on the shelf, several inches further from the edge than before, and went to go lie down, and nobody thought much more of it that night because there was a rocket to check and a station to worry about and, somewhere past the edge of anyone's attention, a ship called the *Shotput* closing distance faster than its published schedule accounted for.

The next morning, Brandon picked up a mug of cold coffee, frowned at it with the particular offense of a man who takes his coffee seriously, and, without quite meaning to, warmed it in his hand until steam rose off the surface.

He looked at the mug for a long moment.

"Sophia," he said, very carefully. "Can you come look at something."

{Aura} began, that same afternoon, a new log category. Not skills, not yet, not formally, just a running list, growing by the week: Jace. Sophia. Now Hayes, whatever he was doing to himself in private. Now, apparently, Brandon. Whoever else the station was quietly making room for.

Nobody had a name for what was happening to Fortuna. {Aura} kept the list anyway, patient and precise, the way she kept everything.
