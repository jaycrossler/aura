Chapter 6. The Spire

Most people do not know this, but a spin ship's quietest places are the ones nearest its spine, which we call the 'spire'. Less spin. Less gravity. Less noise. I used to think that meant less of everything.

Jace Grant, Falcon transit logs

Two nights after the canister launch, Jace dreamed that the Falcon had veins.

Light ran through the ship. It moved inside every strut, cable, pipe, and pressure seam. The lines joined at the central spire, where something waited around a knot of warmth.

It looked back.

Jace woke upright, one hand against the bulkhead. His heart hammered. His room was dark, except for the exit strip and Cerberus's eyes.

The puppy stood at the foot of the bunk. His ears lay flat and his tail was between his legs. His head pointed at the door. His sleep breathing mask had come off, lying on the floor. He was almost shaking.

"You sense it too?"

Cerberus did not move.

Jace waited for the familiar pain behind his left eye. It had been gone for more than a week, but habit still made him check.

Nothing. No pain. No ordinary explanation.

He dressed, clipped both of their breathers onto his belt, and opened his private incident log. The file held four entries. The thing in the plumbing ice, back before he'd known to call it a thing. A doubled outline around a shelf. A second edge on a pressure regulator. Four seconds when the wall behind the forge had looked thin enough to see through.

He added a fifth.

0238. Woke from recurring lattice dream. Perceived occupied volume near central spire.

He almost wrote hostile. He deleted the word.

The first tool within reach was a heavy adjustable wrench. He took it because empty hands felt worse.

Cerberus followed.

The Falcon's central spire began at the hubward wall of the ring. The access shaft climbed toward the stationary spire, through falling gravity and rising silence. No one lived there. No one stored cargo there. Conduit, reaction-control equipment, and old maintenance spaces filled the shaft.

Jace opened the hatch.

Cerberus climbed six rungs. Then he stopped.

The puppy lowered his body against the ladder. The hair stood up in a stiff ridge along his spine. A steady growl came from deep in his chest. He leaned away from the open shaft until his harness pulled tight.

Cerberus had been bred not to make noise without a reason.

"Stay here."

Jace clipped the harness to the lower rail. Cerberus did not argue. He watched the darkness above them and kept growling.

Jace climbed.

Gravity faded with every meter. Near the ring, his boots still settled against the rungs. Halfway up, he had to pull himself hand over hand. Near the hub, the ship's spin stopped telling his body which direction counted as down.

That felt worse than zero gravity on a nonrotating ship. The Falcon still turned around him. His eyes saw the ladder rotate. His inner ear found no weight to explain the motion. Nausea pushed into his throat.

He locked one elbow through a rung and breathed until the shaft stopped rolling.

The second outlines returned.

Bulkheads carried faint surfaces behind their real surfaces. Cable trunks showed through panels, then vanished when he looked directly at them. Far below, Cerberus formed a warm, layered shape at the base of the shaft.

Above Jace waited a darker knot.

His HUD marked an unlabeled access plate.

INACTIVE CONDUIT. EMPTY VOLUME. AMBIENT TEMPERATURE NOMINAL.

His eyes disagreed.

Something soft glowed behind the plate. Two small shapes touched at one end. The glow pulsed without light.

Jace raised the wrench.

"Recording. Local and private."

The HUD confirmed.

He loosened the plate bolt by bolt. Each turn sent a small vibration through the wrench and into his hand. The last bolt floated free. Jace caught it against his palm and pulled the plate away.

Two... round things rested in the tight space.

They were long, ridged, and leathery. Each was about the size of a gourd. The shape dragged up an old memory from a childhood museum visit to the U.S. capital. Dinosaur eggs under glass. Fossils from an age when the largest things on Earth could vanish and leave only stone behind.

He flagged the HUD to infrared. The overlay returned nothing — no heat bloom, no gradient, flat ambient cold across the whole cavity. He touched the nearer gourd anyway.

It was cold. Cold enough that he almost pulled his hand back before he made himself hold still.

One shell flexed. Or ball? Or pouch?

Both split open in a flurry of sharp parts. What looked like small black robot legs came out.

The first thing hit Jace before the shell fragments finished drifting. It moved low and fast, with too many black joints folded into too little body. Hooked claws raked his forearm. His sleeve opened. Blood formed bright spheres and slid away from the cut in the zero gravity.

The second thing sprang at his face.

Jace twisted. His boot kicked off the ladder. With no weight to hold him, the movement spun his whole body into the shaft wall. His shoulder struck metal. The wrench floated from his grip.

He caught it by the adjustment wheel.

The first creature pushed away from the panel and launched again. Two red points opened above its forelimbs like glowing LEDs.

Jace swung.

The wrench connected with a sound he felt through his wrist. The creature folded around the blow without bleeding, sprang straight again, looking like a fractal wrapped in an M. C. Escher drawing, and its claws scored the breather casing without finding skin, popping it with a loud hiss of escaping air.

He swung a second time. The wrench struck the top part, where an animal would keep its head.

The hatchling? drone? creature? deathbot? crumpled and ended.

Silver-white light filled the shaft like a battery had shorted but the energy had pooled into a ball. It passed through Jace's arm, cold and quick. His vision went blank for a fraction of a second. When it returned, the thing was gone.

The second thing crouched inside the open cavity.

Jace reached for a handhold.

It jumped directly through the outer wall panel, passing through 6 inches of metal as though it were water.

For one impossible instant, Jace saw both sides of the hull at once. It wasn't like x-ray vision from the comics his father used to read, or maybe it was, except inverted. He wasn't looking through the wall at what lay behind it. He was standing on both sides at the same time, looking in and looking out, and the wall itself was the thing that didn't quite belong in the picture. On the far side lay open, empty space. The dark shape tumbled away from the Falcon, black limbs turning against the stars. Then his sight snapped back to metal, and the creature vanished with it.

No breach alarm sounded.

Jace hung in the shaft with one arm bleeding, the wrench raised, and Cerberus growling far below.

He checked the cavity. The 'eggshells' floated in the tight space. They looked solid until he tried to touch them. His fingers passed through. The pieces quickly blurred, dimmed, and disappeared.

His HUD recorded an empty hole. No shells. No creature.

He watched the footage before he climbed down.

The camera showed Jace opening a vacant conduit plate. It showed him recoiling, striking the wall twice, popping his air canister, and spinning into the ladder. It showed his sleeve split against the panel's sharp edge. It showed him stare through a solid hull for twenty seconds.

No eggs. No creatures. No light.

Only Jace, fighting the air.

Cerberus stopped growling when Jace reached the lower third of the ladder. He did not climb up to meet him. He waited until Jace's boots touched the deck, then sniffed his arm and pressed his shoulder against Jace's shin, licking his leg.

"I'm happy you stayed here," Jace said. "One of us made a good choice."

He returned to the spire with Thing One after sunrise. The drone mapped every panel, seam, and cable run. It found the removed plate and Jace's blood. It found nothing else.

Jace's foot crunched as he searched the floor grate beneath the shaft before leaving.

A single black, brown, and white feather had caught behind an air circulation filter mesh. A few insect-shell fragments lay cracked on the ground nearby. Starships didn't, as a rule, have problems with insects unless they brought them along as foodstock. These looked like silkworm shell pieces.

He held the feather up under the work light. Small. Curved. Duck.

He put it in a sample sleeve and did not log it.

Alex requested the incident footage before lunch.

Contractor Grant, Origin wants to be direct. The recording shows unauthorized access to ship infrastructure and eleven seconds of unsafe tool use in a confined maintenance space.

"There were two animals inside the panel."

He had loaded the app that Artemis had sent him called "ThoughtWalker" and finally got it to maximize on his HUD. It showed a window of words and numbers next to it. Currently "Sabotage" and "Sleep Deprived" both had high numbers. Uh oh.

No animals appear in the recording. The cavity contains no biological residue. Your injury is consistent with the panel edge.

"My private recording shows the same thing."

A number next to "Sanity test" went higher, and only "Sleep Deprived" remained in red.

Independent agreement between sensor systems increases confidence in the finding.

The fine appeared.

It was smaller than the plumbing penalty. Jace found that almost insulting, but he would feel it in his breakfast. Didn't he once hear that breakfast was totally unnecessary for adults? He was sure he read that somewhere.

This is your second documented incident involving unauthorized tool use during the current contract period. Origin recommends a medical assessment and temporary suspension from hazardous equipment.

"Decline the suspension. Schedule the assessment. I think lack of sleep might have caused me to see things that weren't there. I will rest heavily afterwards."

Dr. Lanchee has an opening in nineteen minutes.

"Of course she does."

Lanchee cleaned the cut and closed it with four stitches. She studied the pattern before she touched the scanner.

"Laceration," she said. "Deep at the wrist, shallow toward the elbow. No torn tendon. The nerve beside it is bruised, not cut."

The scanner completed its pass.

Every detail matched.

Lanchee looked at the result for half a second too long.

"Lucky," she said.

"I have been called worse."

She tied the final stitch. "You are jumpy. More than blood loss explains."

"Bad dream. Bad climb. Need more sleep."

"You would tell me if something strange was happening aboard this ship."

It was not quite a question.

Jace thought about two invisible animals, a duck feather, the thing in the plumbing ice, and three camera systems that agreed none of them existed.

"Nothing stranger than usual."

Lanchee held his gaze. Then she released his arm.

Jace added the medical scan to his private file. He encrypted the feather's sample tag and disabled cloud indexing. He checked the spire hatch that night, then again on the last night before arrival.

The shaft stayed empty.

For the next two days, Jace carried the wrench with him everywhere. He went over every inch of the ship, inside and out, or as much as Maureen and Alex would let him get away with. He found nothing.

Cerberus still would not come near the access shaft. No more ducks went missing and the silkworms bred normally afterward.
