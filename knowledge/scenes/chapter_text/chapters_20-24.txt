Minus nineteen point eight Celsius.

Minus twenty point one.

Outside the station, in deep space, the Astral was not vacuum-cold. It was approximately minus twenty degrees.

"That is survivable," he said.

They mapped the nearby spaces.

The longer a physical volume had existed in one configuration, the warmer its Astral counterpart seemed to be. Rebuilt walls were cold. Old rooms retained more heat. Rooms where people crossed repeatedly were warmer still.

The gas detector found the second problem.

Sophia's Astral room held measurable oxygen, water vapor, and far too much carbon dioxide. Not enough oxygen to breathe safely. More than enough carbon dioxide to make removing a mask a bad idea.

The empty neighboring cabin held much less of all three.

The new service compartment held almost none.

"We did this," Jace said.

Sophia watched the humidity reading climb near the workbench.

Every masked hour had released some moisture and waste gas into a room with no ordinary ventilation. Every breath they had taken while adjusting a mask or swapping a line had remained. Gas also seemed to leak from the physical cabin, but at a rate so slow that construction age showed in the result.

Heat crossed the boundary faster. Air crossed slowly. Human occupancy changed both.

"A room that has been attached for years accumulates more," Sophia said. "New construction begins cold and nearly empty."

"In both places."

"Are you angry because I kept working?"

"You took something from me."

Chapter 21. The Eye on Three

Most people do not know this, but when a machine looks at you, it measures
distance. When something else looks at you, it measures you.

Jace Grant, unpublished notes

Sophia's arm came up before Jace's brain caught up with what had cut him.

Blue white force left her pen in a flat line and struck the creature across what
might have been its shoulder. It recoiled, folding partway back into the wall,
and the tendril that had opened his arm withdrew with it.

Jace had the rod in his other hand. He didn't remember drawing it.

He swung it into the creature's flank. The impact rang up through his wrist and
did almost nothing he could see. The creature barely registered the hit. If
anything, it seemed more interested in Sparky's running lights than in the metal.

That decided it for him.

He dropped the rod and grabbed for Sparky's remote instead.

"Jace—"

"Cover me."

He didn't wait for her to agree. He spun the drone hard, thrusters angled
outward, and drove the throttle to its stop.

Sparky's exhaust caught the creature across its exposed underside at close range.
It should not have mattered against something built out of wet tissue and folded
space. It mattered anyway. The creature convulsed, and the sound that came out of
it wasn't a mechanical failure tone or an animal scream. It was something that
used the Astral itself to carry, a howl that arrived behind Jace's eyes instead of
through his ears, and both of them staggered under it.

It fled back toward the wall it had come from.

"Go," Sophia said, and this time he didn't argue.

They ran for the stairway connecting Ring Two down toward the transfer point,
Sparky clutched against Jace's chest, his arm and side burning in a way that had
stopped being pain and started being something colder.

The creature followed them as far as the first step.

It stopped there. Reared back, actually, the way an animal balks at a threshold
it will not cross, and Jace, half blind with shock, noticed the thin bright line
worked into the stair rail where it met the deck.

Silver.

He filed it next to copper and kept moving.

By the second flight his legs weren't reliable anymore. Sophia got a shoulder
under his good arm and half carried him the rest of the way, saying nothing,
breathing hard, not letting go.

They crossed back into the Victoria together, badly, the way they always
crossed back badly now.



The physical cabin hit him like a dropped weight. Cold first, then the ordinary
gravity of the ship, then Sophia's voice very close to his ear telling him to
stay with her.

He couldn't see properly. The room had gone soft at the edges, and he was
shaking in a way that had nothing to do with temperature.

"I can't, everything's—" He didn't finish the sentence. He wasn't sure what word
belonged at the end of it.

Misty's processor cabinet threw more light into the room than anything else in
it, the ordinary steady green white glow of a working stack. Sophia steered him
toward it because it was close and because, later, she would not be able to
explain any better reason than that.

She set him down against it to catch her breath.

Jace's shoulder settled against the casing.

He felt it the way he had felt Sophia's light the first time. A pull, immediate
and enormous, gold brown warmth flooding up through contact he hadn't chosen and
didn't understand until it was already happening.

"Jace!"

He couldn't let go. For three, four seconds, he didn't want to.

Sophia hauled him sideways by the collar, breaking the contact.

Somewhere above them, Misty's cabin lighting dimmed by half.

Jace sat on the floor of the Victoria, bleeding, shaking, and abruptly,
humiliatingly, awake. Alert. His hands had stopped being numb. The cold that had
been eating him from the inside since Ring Three had a name now, and the name was
that it had gone somewhere else.

"I didn't mean to," he said.

"I know."

"I felt her go dark and I couldn't stop, I couldn't make my hand move—"

"I know, Jace."

Sophia was looking at the cabinet, not at him, both hands pressed flat against
the casing.

Misty did not answer the room.

"This is not your fault," Jace said. "I sat down there. You didn't—"

"I put you there." Sophia's voice was very level. "I chose the brightest thing in
the room because you couldn't see straight, and I didn't think about what the
brightness was."

"You didn't know."

"I am not interested in whether I knew." She finally looked at him. Not angry.
Something worse than angry. "Is this what it's doing? On Three? Is it doing to
people, or to systems, what you just did to her?"

Jace didn't have an answer.

Neither did she. She didn't try to invent one.

"Rest," she said instead. "We decide what to do about any of this in the
morning."



Lanchee's emergency kit was not built for this much of Jace at once, but it was
enough. Stitches in his forearm, a wrap around his ribs, strict orders to sleep
that he had no intention of following.

He sat on his bunk with Cerberus's weight against his good leg and a data pad
balanced on his knee, making lists, because lists were the only thing his hands
would still do that felt like control.

Rod. Ineffective against tissue at that density. Redesign or discard.

Sparky thruster exhaust against organic tissue at close range. Effective. Document
exact distance and duration once memory stabilizes.

Silver. Second material of interference, independent of copper. Do not assume
equivalence between the two.

He was ashamed of how good he felt while writing it. His arm hurt. His side hurt
worse. And underneath all of it sat an unfamiliar, unwelcome clarity, less than
what he'd taken from Sophia that first night, but enough that he could sit
upright and think in full sentences instead of static.

He had taken it from Misty. He kept circling back to that fact the way a tongue
finds a broken tooth.

His comm chimed, station traffic log, a channel he mostly ignored, except that
Kim's voice came through it clipped and irritated.

"—don't care whose survey window it was, I had to clear four drones out of that
lane at 0200 because somebody scheduled an unscheduled launch and didn't tell
traffic control. Jin's crew is gone. Long survey run, minimum a week, and I found
out about it from a collision alert."

A week. Jace set the pad down.

There would be no talking to Jin, no talking to anyone on that crew, about the
unlisted room, about what they might or might not have to do with the thing that
had cut him open. Not for a week. Possibly longer.

He looked at his list again. Do not assume equivalence.

He added one more line underneath the others.

Nobody to ask. Find out on our own.

Chapter 22. Quiet Companion

Most people do not know this, but the best assistant is not the one that speaks first. It is the one that notices the instrument was wrong before you build a life around the answer.

Jace Grant, unpublished notes

Jace did not cross the next day.

His hands recovered temperature slowly. Hot water felt sharp. Cold water felt like pressure. Sophia recorded both findings, then refused his suggestion that they repeat the exposure to establish a curve.

"One encounter with a creature is not an exposure study," she said.

"It is one data point."

"It tried to reach into Sparky's light."

"A memorable data point."

Sophia closed the notebook.

"We need better instruments."

"The kind I cannot build without Nick."

"Then you must decide which danger is larger. Telling your friend, or continuing to lie to him badly."

"I have not lied."

She looked at him.

"I have omitted the impossible parts."

"That sentence is a lie wearing a technical uniform."

Jace went to the forge.

Nick was in the forge when Jace arrived.

He sat behind the partition with Jace's diagnostics open on three screens. Green hair covered one lens of his cracked overlay glasses. The pearl earring below his left ear pulsed with muted light.

"I know."

"She asked me to make sure you did not install anything clever while unsupervised."

"That feels targeted."

Nick did not smile.

"I need your help."

"No."

"You do not know what I am asking."

"I know it concerns sensor failures, Sophia, and the hours you keep disappearing. I know you will give me a cover story and ask me to certify it. No."

Jace had prepared six explanations. All of them failed before he spoke.

"I am asking you to help me with something I am not ready to explain," he said. "That is unfair. You were right about the instruments, and you were right about me being somewhere else."

Nick's keyboard gloves went still.

"Is someone in danger?"

Jace thought of the red eye fixed on him and the fine threads reaching toward Sparky's light.

"Yes."

"Sophia?"

"Potentially. Cerberus. Me. Maybe more people, but I do not know yet."

"Is this military?"

"No."

"Illegal?"

"Not intentionally."

"That is not comforting."

"It is accurate."

Nick removed his glasses and cleaned them on his shirt. Without the overlays, his face looked younger and much more tired.

"A local system layer that can compare raw sensor streams before the station services normalize them. It has to run quiet, keep the data local, and tell me when two instruments disagree before one of them gets voted out."

"I want you to stick your baby in my brain."

Nick shut his eyes.

"Not imagery that I needed."

"You said they were safe."

"This is not a deal between rival corporations."

Lanchee performed the installation in the Ring One clinic.

The room had once been a Spoke storage compartment. Its cabinets still matched the ship's curved walls. A folded privacy screen covered the place where Jace had once hidden a canister labeled PIG BUTTS.

"Full operating-system backup?" she asked.

"Complete," Nick said. "Offline copy in the clinic store. Second encrypted copy in his personal locker."

"Rollback test?"

"Passed."

"Minimum set. No autonomous purchasing, no medical changes, no external data sharing."

Lanchee looked at Jace.

"You understand that any personalized system becomes more useful by observing you. You can revoke access. Revoking access may reduce function."

"I understand."

"Say it without trying to finish faster."

Jace read the statement on the screen and consented to each permission separately.

Nick placed the transfer patch behind Jace's ear. Cold spread along the implant seam.

The setup interface opened across his vision.

Three avatar options appeared: male, female, and a neutral geometric figure.

"That one looks like a loading error," Jace said.

"It is popular with engineers," Nick said.

Aura: Confirmed.

Aura: Your recent behavior contains periods of high cognitive load, reduced sleep, elevated physical stress, and incomplete task closure. I recommend mostly quiet companion mode. I will answer direct questions, flag urgent safety conflicts, and defer nonurgent observations to a daily review.

"You trained it to insult me."

Aura: One deferred personal item may be time-sensitive. A message from Artemis Grant contains a grammatically complete sentence with a discontinuity in semantic content. Packet integrity is valid.

"I blame evidence after I have it."

It was the correct answer. It did not make Jace feel better.



The next morning, Aura did not greet him.

Jace approved.

Aura: Two.

She answered before the first tone ended.

"It was not breaking the instruments," he said. "It was giving them two places to be."




Chapter 23. No Vampires

Most people do not know this, but the first rule of discovering a new form of energy is not “measure it.” The first rule is “agree not to steal it from anybody.” Then measure it.

Jace Grant, unpublished notes

The first version of the proposal began in Sophia's room three days before they
were ready to show it to anyone.

Three cameras on Sparky could capture incompatible projections of the same Astral
subject. An MRI or computed-tomography scan could provide a physical model for
comparison. An Aura model might eventually learn the transformation between
them. The first experiments had supplied the idea, two accidental drains, and
several reasons not to place the equipment near another person.

Nick had supplied isolated sensor feeds, independent clocks, and an Aura
instance capable of flagging contradictions without inventing a single corrected
answer. He still did not know where the feeds went.

It did not supply consent.

That required Lanchee.



Lanchee read the proposed experiment in silence.

Jace and Sophia sat across from her in the clinic office. Cerberus lay under Jace's chair with his chin across both boots. Since sketching the first version, they had drained Sophia, nearly shut down the Victoria, found a creature in a wall, and built a sensor package Nick still did not understand. Jace's hands worked again, although cold water still felt like pressure and hot water felt like an accusation.

The proposal on Lanchee's screen did not mention the Astral.

Sophia had called the device a remote sensing array. The comparison section described magnetic resonance imaging, ground-penetrating radar, ultrasound, and lidar, then admitted that the new method behaved like none of them. It could operate from a nearby room. It appeared to show internal structure through walls. Its output contained more information than the physical sensors sent into it.

They had tested it on printed blocks, ore samples, sections of asteroid, Sparky, and themselves. The results were repeatable enough to be interesting and contradictory enough to be dangerous.

Now Sophia wanted to aim it at known injuries.

"If the correlation holds," she said, "the method may identify structural changes that existing medical imaging misses. I intend to publish the instrumentation, error model, and privacy limits as a paper after independent review."

Lanchee reached the final page.

"No," she said.

Sophia leaned forward. "Which provision concerns you?"

"You will give me every record. You will not repeat this on another patient without me. You will not describe it as safe."

"Agreed," Jace said.

"And you will tell me what you are calling the structure."

Jace looked at the spiral he had drawn from memory.

"A leech."

Sophia shook her head.

"Too imprecise. Its mouth was dominated by repeated compressive geometry. A tick, perhaps."

"A Tick-Maw."

She considered it.

"Still imprecise. Memorable, though."

Jace wrote the name beneath the drawing.

The first line under it was not a description of the creature.

It was the cost.

One patient recovering. One physical injury. One unknown absorption.

Below that, he wrote the next requirement.

Find out how many more are aboard Fortuna.
