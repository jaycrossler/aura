Chapter 22. No Vampires

Most people do not know this, but the first rule of discovering a new form of energy is not “measure it.” The first rule is “agree not to steal it from anybody.” Then measure it.

Jace Grant, unpublished notes

Lanchee read the proposed experiment in silence.

Jace and Sophia sat across from her in the clinic office. Cerberus lay under Jace's chair with his chin across both boots. They had not crossed since the creature in the wall. Jace's hands worked again, although cold water still felt like pressure and hot water felt like an accusation.

The proposal on Lanchee's screen did not mention the Astral.

It described a study of unexplained sensor disagreement near medical equipment. It requested two volunteer patients, one empty treatment room, access to anonymized biometric trends, and permission to operate an isolated camera package for twenty minutes.

Everything in it was true.

It was also missing the impossible parts.

Lanchee reached the final page.

"No," she said.

Sophia leaned forward. "Which provision concerns you?"

"The absence of a purpose. You list six things you wish to measure and no physical mechanism connecting them. You request patients but say the test will not interact with them. You request medical trends but cannot tell me what variable you expect to change."

"We are characterizing an observation method," Sophia said.

"For what observation?"

Sophia's fingers tightened around her father's pen.

Jace answered first.

"We found a sensor environment we do not understand. It may reveal structure that ordinary cameras miss. It may also create false positives. We want to find out which before we point it at anything dangerous."

"Where is this environment?"

"Adjacent to the station."

Lanchee looked at him.

"That is an evasive answer."

"Yes."

"Is the experiment dangerous to the volunteers?"

"We do not think so."

"That is not sufficient."

"They will not cross the boundary or touch the equipment. Sophia and I take all exposure. If either of us shows a medical change, we stop. If a volunteer shows any change outside normal treatment, we stop and give you the complete data."

"You will give me the complete data in every case."

Jace glanced at Sophia.

She nodded.

"Every case," he said.

Lanchee added conditions. No patient names in their records. No continuous image storage. No procedure during acute care. A clinic observer would remain in the room. Any unexplained physical effect ended the study. She would choose the volunteers and describe the test to them as experimental sensor calibration.

"That description is incomplete," she said, "but it is not false. If I learn that you concealed a risk, there will not be a second experiment."

"Agreed," Jace said.

"There may not be a first. I have not said yes."

She turned the screen around.

"Explain data deletion."

Jace pointed to the local buffer. "The physical camera overwrites after ten minutes unless you preserve a segment. The transferred camera stores only differences between frames. Aura keeps timestamps and my verbal markers, not neural imagery."

"Can your AI infer identity from the markers?"

"Not if we remove time, room, and treatment type."

"Then remove them before analysis."

Sophia frowned. "Treatment type is a useful variable."

"It is also enough to identify a patient when my clinic has only one fracture. You may retain injury class after I approve the category."

Sophia considered that, then drew a line through a field on the form.

"Agreed."

Lanchee looked at Jace. "Useful data is not automatically data you have a right to keep."

He thought of walking through Fortuna while every private room had been reduced to a visible plane.

"Understood."

She called up the volunteer list.

"One patient has a healing wrist fracture. The other is recovering from a pressure bruise. Both are stable. Ten minutes each. You remain in the empty examination room."

Sophia allowed herself one breath of relief.

"Thank you, Doctor."

"Do not thank me until I see your equipment."



They rebuilt the Astral package around the failure.

Sparky carried the camera Jace had transferred across in pieces. The physical side used a second camera, two independent clocks, a suit diagnostic puck, and Aura's isolated buffer. Copper wire connected every component to a paper tag bearing a handwritten number. No wireless discovery. No shared timing. No cooperative correction.

It looked less like advanced science than a collection of appliances under investigation for a crime.

Nick inspected it without touching anything.

"The clocks will drift," he said.

"That is the point."

"The camera will drop frames."

"Also the point."

"The physical package cannot see wherever you are going."

"It can see what my body does while I am there."

Nick looked at the sealed door to the Victoria cabin where Sophia waited.

"You are still not telling me."

"No."

"But you told the doctor there was a boundary."

"Yes."

"Progress," Nick said without warmth. "Small, irritating progress."

He checked Aura's permissions. The local instance could timestamp Jace's reported observations, compare them with his implant and suit telemetry, and flag contradictions. It could not transmit raw content, query station systems, or change medical settings.

"Your skull temperature rises when it processes," Nick said. "If it reaches the yellow line, you stop."

"Lanchee already said that."

"Now two people are correct."

He left before the crossing.

Jace watched the door close.

Sophia set out the bread and water. "He helped."

"He is still angry."

"Both can be true."

"You enjoy that answer too much."

"It remains efficient."

Crossing 53 took them into the cold.



The clinic did not resemble the clinic.

Its physical walls had flattened into overlapping planes. Cabinets became pale frames with no depth. Metal instruments persisted as faint edges. Wood, paper, and some plastics stood out with more certainty. The folded privacy screen looked stronger than the wall supporting it.

Beyond the examination room, people burned.

Jace had seen human light through Fortuna before, but distance and fear had reduced it to color. Here, within a few meters, every person contained motion too dense for sight.

One patient shone blue-white at the center and green along the limbs. Gold threads moved through both colors. Darker shapes formed and dissolved with each breath. The light did not stop at the skin. It folded inward through patterns that seemed larger than the body holding them.

Sophia stared.

"Rainbows in human heads," she whispered.

"That is your scientific description?"

"My scientific description will require several more notebooks."

Cerberus glowed beside them, simpler and warmer, his attention a bright direction that followed every movement in the ward. Jace looked down at his own hands. Color moved beneath them in branching streams.

A new shape rested behind his eyes.

It was not a face. It had no human color. Fine geometric lines opened and closed in repeating layers, each joined to Jace's glow by hundreds of hair-thin connections.

Sophia stepped closer.

"Your AI," she said.

"You can see it?"

"I can see a structure that was not there before. It is very orderly."

Jace addressed the local instance aloud even though sound did not travel normally here.

"Aura, mark. New visual structure associated with local processing. Sophia observes it too."

No answer came through the Astral.

Back in his physical body, the implant recorded the words his nervous system attempted to form. Aura added a timestamp.

The geometric light shifted.

Sophia followed the connections with her eyes.

"It is using your structure," she said. "Not consuming it. The lines brighten when you pay attention to it, then settle."

"Unspent capacity," Jace said.

"Do not name it after one observation."

"Temporary label. Unspent Will."

"You named it Will?"

"It is short."

"It is philosophy pretending to be a unit."

"Then you will enjoy correcting me for years."

The first volunteer entered the treatment room on the physical side. A construction technician with a healing wrist fracture sat where Lanchee directed. The cast did not exist here as a solid object. Its fibers formed a pale cage around the arm.

Inside that cage, green light moved.

It gathered at the fracture in slow waves, flowed through the break, and returned along the arm. Each pass left the damaged area a little more ordered.

Sophia stopped arguing.

"It follows blood flow," Jace said.

"Not exactly. Some crosses against it."

"Nerve paths?"

"Not exactly."

"You are using my favorite answer."

They watched for eight minutes. They did not touch the patient. They did not move closer. Jace marked observations while Aura compared his attempted speech, pulse, implant load, and the physical room's clocks.

The green light intensified when the patient flexed her fingers. Her physical monitor showed a small rise in heart rate and no other change.

When she left, Sophia wrote for three full minutes.

"If Will can be directed toward damage," she said, "then it may be possible to increase healing."

"Or decrease it."

"Yes."

"And if the light is a reserve, it might be transferable."

Sophia's pen stopped.

Jace looked through the flattened wall at the people in the clinic. Some were brighter than others. A child in the corridor carried a warm yellow corona. An exhausted nurse had almost none beyond the light bound close to her body.

The discovery changed shape.

It was no longer about seeing.

It was about access.

"No taking it," Jace said.

"Agreed."

"Not from a person. Not to prove it can move. Not because we think they have more than they need."

"Agreed."

"No changing anything inside someone without informed consent and medical supervision."

"Yes."

"No recording people through walls. No identifying observations unless they consented."

Sophia looked toward the volunteer who had already left.

"Our view exceeded what she understood."

"Then we delete the images and keep only the measurements Lanchee approved."

"And tell her the method revealed more than expected."

"Yes."

Sophia wrote a heading across a clean page.

Human Observation Protocol.

Under it, Jace added a shorter line.

We are not going to be vampires.

Sophia read it.

"This is not formal language."

"It is memorable language."

She left it on the page.

A nurse crossed the corridor outside the physical room.

In the Astral, the wall did not hide her. Her light entered before her body reached the doorway and remained after she passed. A sharp violet knot flared near her chest when she paused beside the isolation ward. Gold threads tightened around it. Jace could not hear her or see her expression, but he could see that something in the room had changed her.

He turned away.

"That was not part of the study," he said.

Sophia lowered her pen. "No."

"The camera caught it."

They checked Sparky's buffer. The image was mostly noise, but six frames held the nurse's colored outline clearly enough to distinguish her from the patients.

"Delete those frames," Sophia said.

Jace marked them. Aura confirmed the deletion against both local checksums. No image remained, but the act did not restore the privacy they had already crossed.

"Walls create consent boundaries in the physical world," Sophia said. "Here they do not."

"Then we have to create them ourselves."

They repositioned behind the folded privacy screen. The screen's ordered fibers blocked part of their view more effectively than the metal wall. Jace noted the effect, then stopped.

"We cannot turn privacy into another materials test while someone is using it."

Sophia closed that page of the notebook.

For the remaining observation period, they faced only the marked chair. When clinic staff moved outside the approved area, Jace closed his eyes. It felt absurd to use eyelids in a place where his body did not work normally. It also worked.

The fracture patient's remaining data showed the green activity repeating at a slower rate as she rested. Aura found a matching reduction in heart rate, but no causal direction. Sophia dictated three possible explanations and rejected all three as premature.

"We have proven almost nothing," she said.

"We proved there is something worth being careful about."

"That is not nothing."



The second volunteer never arrived.

Lanchee entered the physical examination room and ended the session. Her voice reached Jace only as a distant pressure through his body.

Aura converted the implant signal into a marker: MEDICAL INTERRUPTION. RETURN REQUESTED.

Jace and Sophia crossed back.

Warmth, weight, and nausea struck together. Jace opened his eyes on the cabin floor. Cerberus licked his face until he pushed the dog away.

Before Lanchee spoke, the first volunteer asked to see them.

The construction technician stood in the doorway with her cast held against her chest. Lanchee had told her that the method revealed more biological detail than the consent form described and that the unapproved images had been deleted.

"Did you see something wrong with my arm?" she asked.

Sophia answered. "We observed activity concentrated around the fracture. We do not know what it is or whether it changes healing. Your physical measurements remained normal."

"Activity like radiation?"

"No evidence of radiation. The closest honest answer is that we do not have a category for it."

The woman looked from Sophia to Jace.

"Was I in danger?"

"We saw no sign of danger," Jace said. "We also saw more of you than we had permission to see. The camera frames are gone. The remaining record says only adult volunteer, healing fracture, no physical change."

"Can it see through clothes?"

"It can see through walls," Jace said.

Sophia glanced at him, but did not soften the answer.

The technician absorbed that.

"That seems like something people should know."

"Yes," Sophia said.

"Are you going to tell them?"

Jace thought of Nick outside the hatch and Suzi's cameras across Fortuna. A public warning without a repeatable demonstration would sound like paranoia. Silence would protect their work by leaving everyone else ignorant of a privacy risk.

"We need evidence that can survive someone else testing it," he said. "Until then, the method does not leave our control, and we do not point it at anyone who has not agreed."

"That was not my question."

"No," Jace said. "It was not."

Sophia closed her notebook.

"If the result repeats, we tell the medical and station authorities what the method can expose before we publish how to reproduce it."

The technician looked at Lanchee.

"You believe them?"

"I believe they encountered something their form did not describe," Lanchee said. "I believe they deleted your images. I have not decided what the observation means."

That answer seemed to satisfy her more than reassurance would have.

"Keep the anonymous measurements," she said. "If you learn whether the green thing helps the bone, Doctor Lanchee can tell me."

Sophia wrote the added consent in front of her and gave her a copy.

Only after the volunteer left did Lanchee turn to the interruption.

She pointed through the hatch toward the medical bay.

"One of the Chinese miners was transferred here from their medical section," she said. "He has been unstable for twelve days. The attending system classifies the trend as recovery, but the man continues to weaken."

"Why bring him now?" Sophia asked.

"Because the transfer summary mentions the same sensor disagreement you described."

Jace sat up.

"What injury?"

"A wound from shoulder to ribs."

The cafeteria returned to him. A Filipino miner leaning over his noodles. Massive wound. Suit intact. Somehow no pressure loss.

"No breach," Jace said.

Lanchee's eyes narrowed. "How do you know that?"

"I heard about it during my first week."

"The tissue closed. There is no active bleeding and no infection. His blood volume is stable. He eats, but loses weight. He sleeps, but does not recover. Every model says he should improve."

Sophia opened her notebook.

"Has his implant load changed?"

"Intermittent spikes. No process claims them."

"Where is he?" Jace asked.

"Isolation room two. You are not entering it."

"Understood."

Lanchee studied both of them.

"Can your observation method see through a wall?"

Jace thought of the human light spread through the clinic. He thought of the creature folding around Fortuna's cables.

"Yes."

"Can the patient be harmed by it?"

"Not by looking."

"That answer contains more confidence than your proposal."

"We have one additional observation."

Lanchee's mouth tightened.

"Five minutes. No intervention. If you see anything, you return and tell me before you act."

Sophia answered before Jace could.

"Agreed."

They crossed again from the empty room.

The miner's light was easy to find.

It should have filled isolation room two.

Instead it clung close to his body, thin and gray at the edges.

Something broad and amber lay across his back.

Its flattened body covered the wound from shoulder to ribs. Tendrils passed through the man's neck and spine. With each pulse, color moved out of him and into the thing.

Sophia caught Jace's arm before he stepped forward.

"We promised to return," she said.

The parasite raised its head.

Its mouth opened like a spiral.
