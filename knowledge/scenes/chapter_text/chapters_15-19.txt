Chapter 15. Sideways

Most people do not know this, but entering another dimension on purpose begins like most bad decisions. Dinner. One glass of wine. Then someone asks whether you want to go back to their place.

Jace Grant, unpublished notes

Sophia finished her coffee, then produced a small sealed bottle from her bag.

Jace read the label.

"Trittenheimer Apotheke. Real wine from Germany."

"One glass helps me relax. Not enough to impair me. Enough to make the transition easier."

"Your dimensional travel protocol includes alcohol."

"I have six notebooks of evidence. You have one bad night and a dog. And, the wine is getting old. Past ten years, it's not drinkable."

"Fourty glasses of wine at five glassed per bottle is eight bottles. That's got to cost a fortune."

"I'm German, we are efficient... we drink 4 glasses per bottle. And we have a wine cellar on the Victoria. Brandon is our chemist. He is constantly brewing more. We have a whole room for it. Smells amazing."

"Space wine? Wait, we're getting off track. We were discussing if we go back."

Cerberus put one paw on Sophia's knee.

"When we go back. Looks like two votes," she said.

"He does not vote."

Cerberus moved the paw higher.

"He is very expressive. And a smart boy."

Sophia poured a measured glass and drank half. She sealed the rest.

"Now," she said, "your place or mine?"

Jace stopped with the fork of noodles halfway to his mouth.

Sophia held the expression for three seconds. Then she laughed.

"I want a room without station cameras. Mine has better air and fewer corporate microphones."

"You could have led with that."



The Victoria occupied a docking cradle on the outer edge of Ring Two. From the cooridor, you couldn't tell there was a ship behind the door. It and the five doors nearest it looked just like any other inner hatchway, though each had been decorated with a brick wall with a flower pot outside and a little porch, like doors to townhouses back on Earth. Bricks, it turned out, were pretty easy to make when you were mining asteroids all day.

You walk through the outside door, down a short hallway, and then to the Victoria's airlock. Jake guessed that the ship could undock, be tugged down the spine and launched from the shuttle pad, probably within hours if needed. It was double the size of Torres's survey ship, and looked like it could (and had) lasted years in the black.

He found it amazing how Fortuna blended all these ways to live in space and made them... convenient.

Inside, it was a thirty-meter long ESA science vessel built for a few scientists, a small laboratory, and no wasted volume. Its hull carried old micrometeor repairs in three different alloys. A blue and gold ESA mission emblem had faded beside the door lock.

The air inside smelled like warm herbs, yeast, and fruit. Muted tans and browns and natural greens blended with pops of color and wood-paneled walls. It was the closest thing to home Jace had smelled since Virginia, and he almost teared up.

Cerberus kept sniffing everything, nose pumping like a bellows. His tail wagged at light speed. AS they enetered, he smelled other subtle scents; lavender and maybe sage. It was such a contrast from the sweat and dust of Toress' ship. The Victoria felt more like an old pub than a spaceship.

"Welcome aboard," said a molodic voice from the corridor speakers. "You must be Jace. Sophia mentioned you might stop by."

"Hi," Jace said, looking around, seeing no other women. He looked at Sophia quizzickly. She just smirked.

"Misty," Sophia said. "She is our fourth crewmate."

Jace glanced at the nearest speaker. "Ship AI?"

"European AI, and entirely local. She runs on her own server stack aboard the Victoria. No station servers, no station sync."

"Misty, you can bother Jace later. Let him come aboard in peace."

Sophia's shipmates waited in the central cabin. It had couches and was arranged around a large communal counter. The room was clean, colorful, and cozy.

"This is Brandon. He makes the wine."

The French man raised a drink to them, looking from Sophia to Jace, then down at Cerberus. He whistled and Cerberus trotted over to receive head scratches.

"And Nikos. He's Greek so forgive him in advance."

Nikos smirked, eyebrow quirked. Jace remembered that he'd delivered him some printed beakers last week.

"This is Jace. He's an engineer. Helping me with instrument calibration," Sophia said.

Both of Sophia's crewmates grinned. Lecherously.

"It is private," she added.

That did not improve the grins.

Sophia pushed Jace toward the aft passage, swearing at them. Cerberus followed, still sniffing the air.

She muttered, "Scientists are terrible people."

Jace replied, "But you're a scientist."

"This is how I know."

Her cabin was wide and orderly. Every object had a place. Scarves hung from hooks in a line of bright color. Paper journals filled a shelf, textured to look like an oak bookcase. A small Christmas image had been fixed above the desk. A large screen showed calculations and maps and pinned notes. A box on the screen was green, its microphone icon blinking.

It felt lived in where his room at the inn felt issued.

"Misty."

"Yes, Sophia?"

"Can you start a new protocol? When Jace enters here with me, turn off all monitoring until we leave. Full privacy mode."

Misty sounded mischievous: "Whatever you say. Have fun, you two." The box on the screen turned red, with the words 'Privacy Mode' showing in English, German, and French.

Sophia opened a wood-paneled shelf and pulled out a round loaf of fresh dark bread.

"I made this as a thank-you gift. We have an oven."

Jace tried a slice. She pulled out a tub of butter and some of the cheese she had put in her handbag from dinner. It was the best thing he'd eaten since Mars. It was fluffy, the lower gravity making huge airy bubbles in the dough. Like a thicker brioche, covered in seeds. The heavenly scent woke him up even more than the coffee had. He was ravenous.

"This is actual bread. It's divine."

"Thank you. Your standards must be slipping."

"I'm used to soup."

"Yes. I have noticed your tragedy. Hopefully some bread will improve the taste of your soup."

Cerberus sat beneath where she had placed the rest of the loaf, slobbering and drooling over her nice soft wood-tone floors.

Sophia cut him a piece and handed it over, smeared with a bit of cheese. Then she scratched his neck with both hands.

"He looks like he's turning in his notice and defecting to the Victoria."

"Any Mops Spitz is welcome to defect! They are very popular in Germany."

"We call them Puggle mixes. Bred on Mars to have short hair. They are supposed to be less vocal but he and his sister don't seem to have gotten that message."

"He's wonderful."

Cerberus finished eating, then saw they were both watching him. Showtime. He twirled, trying to nip at his tail. He fell over in the low gravity onto her foot, legs up in the air. His expressive face raised his cheek muscles, a giant puppy smile marred only by a lolling tongue getting in the way. Sophia laughed into his neck. Cerberus sneezed, jumped up, spun once, and curled into a ball, watching them unguardedly.

The tension left Sophia's shoulders.

Jace understood that the dog had accomplished in twenty seconds what a glass of wine and an hour of conversation had not. In a flash of rare insight, Jace realized she must have been worried that he wouldn't help. Or that he'd be weird when alone in her quarters. Despite his size, or because of the two men outside, she must have concluded he was non-threatening enough to work with.

Sophia stood, taking on a professorial tone.

"OK. Masks first. Full cartridges with backup air. No metal. If one person signals return, all three return. Once there, we pull a few things across, starting with a timer. Then we time everything we can."

She handed him a written checklist.

That was the most reassuring thing she had said all evening.

Sophia opened a fresh notebook page and wrote a large number at the top.

42.

"Crossing number?" Jace asked.

"Last night was 41. This is our first planned crossing together. An auspicious number."



Sophia went first and took fifteen minutes to disappear.

It took Jace and Cerberus more than twenty minutes. He had to relax, almost meditate, and ignore the breathing woman next to him.

Cerberus was beside Jace with one paw raised, as if he had stepped over something too small to see.

The Victoria unfolded around them.

Its pressure hull curved near and far at once. Jace saw the lab, drive spaces, water tanks, docking clamps, and the station beyond. Physical metal became layered darkness edged with light. Warm physical air moved through him without touching him.

Cold settled through his clothes.

His mask thermometer stabilized near minus twenty Celsius.

"Cold," he said.

"Yes."

"I wore two layers."

She had reassembled her pen and wrote something on the checklist. "Next time, let's try three."

They explored her ship first. Her room on the Victoria looked almost completely different. Her shelves here were covered in bottles, beakers, notebooks, and other things. Trash was kept in a box, presumably as wrappers and packaging took time to move across the barrier.

Jace kept one hand on Cerberus's harness as they walked. Sophia asked him to name each observation as they moved. She scribbled furiously in a new notebook, sometimes uttering "Merde" or "Scheisse" when he pointed out something she hadn't realized before.

"Physical metal is visible but not fully solid. Copper feels almost twice as weak as aluminum."

"Hmm."

Jace reached and pulled across the small timing module he brought. It was a very simple plastic lab timer, less than a Token to print.

He pulled, and she counted. It felt as though the outer plastic shell came easily across, but the circuit board and display got "stuck" in space. That part took longer, but he breathed and pulled. It felt like going to the gym, lifting weights on a Bowflex machine. Finally, the rest snapped through.

"Fifteen minutes and a few seconds."

He was already sweating despite the cold. She wrote down the numbers, then pushed beeping buttons. Everything seemed to work normally.

Sophia was giddy with excitement. "But now we can time it more accurately!"

He smiled at her enthusiasm. That beeping might get old quickly. He resolved to print a new timer with a smaller screen and no beep. Origin was sure to have different schematics.

"The floor feels solid. Or at least, the lower layer which is graphite. The steel above it lets me easily sink a finger through it."

"The floor is solid until it does not. My foot almost fell through once. I don't let myself think that way anymore."

"I see your friends outside, glowing. But what is over there? Your ship's reactor is bright, and so are the water tanks. But the thing behind the aft lab is brighter than the reactor. Way brighter."

"Misty's processor stack," Sophia said.

They walked through walls to look at things. Cerberus didn't like that, and had to be carried, whining.

The reactor shone white-gold through twenty meters of folded ship. Behind it, a compact tower burned like a lantern set in ice. They approached and saw thousands of tiny brown-gold bubbles formed around it like sea foam, folding into geometric chains and vanishing before he could follow one chain fully. It was ethereally beautiful. He felt an urge to scoop up the bubbles and see how they tasted.

"Complexity may matter. Energy may matter. Both may matter. I need more measurements. We need a way to measure distance. And these bubbles."

"You have six notebooks of measurements."

"Sieben now." She reverted to German when she was deep in concentration.

They walked through walls back to the galley. Sophia's shipmates sat at a workbench. Both glowed through skin, clothing, and chair. Nikos said something and laughed, then turned and kissed Brandon.

Jace heard nothing, but raised his eyebrows. Sophia just shrugged. "We've been shipmates for almost five years. They are family."

Sophia leaned close to the table.

"I think I feel the vibration when they speak. We need a high-precision microphone."

Jace placed his palm where the physical surface appeared. Something trembled at the edge of perception, too faint for rhythm.

Brandon reached for a cup. His hand passed through Jace's arm. Jace could see both the cup and the liquid. And what looked like glowing fractals in Brandon's head.

Neither man reacted.

Jace stepped back.

"We could walk into any room on the station."

"Yes."

"Watch any conversation. Read any screen."

"Without hearing it. And you can't make out details on screens." She pointed to the one on the wall which just looked like a gray rectangle, then a hand-carved cuckoo clock. "We can make out analogue clock hands."

"That does not make it any less of a security and ethical quandry."

"No. Ethics remain a problem."

They stayed aboard the Victoria. They pulled across more equipment while she timed and measured everything. A quick calculation showed that he could pull things four times faster than she could, and working together helped. It was close, physical, difficult work, and they both reveled in the discovery, novelty, and science.

They looked at bubbling vats of grape juice and yeast in a rear temperature-controlled closet. Thermometers, humidity gauges, and glassware surrounded the vats, barely visible as multidimensional outlines. The yeast was bright inside the vats. It would make a lot of wine. Sophia told him that the farm on Ring Two had grape vines that were technically owned by the ESA and had been growing for decades, so any ESA ship that asked received a monthly allocation of grape juice.

They moved about the ship and pulled in more air canisters, food, the bread. Blankets. Pillows. Sophia would order more, until Jace said he would print them to keep them off any suspicious logs. She made lists and plans and notes.

It was enough for one night. More than enough. It was miraculous.

The ship had become a country with impossible borders. They walked into her lab and adjoining room without opening its hatch. They stood inside a water tank and watched physical liquid pass through their bodies. They looked out through every side of the hull at once.

Cerberus discovered he could chase a reflection that belonged to a direction humans did not have. Not up or sideways, but something else that they couldn't quite understand.

He lost.



They returned with more than an hour of air remaining in their breathers and much more saved for the next journey.

The cabin snapped into three dimensions. Warmth touched Jace's face. His stomach stayed behind for one terrible second, then caught up.

Sophia wrote until her hand cramped.

Jace slept nine hours that night, the happiest deepest sleep he had had in months.



They agreed to keep exploring, jobs permitting. Sophia was on a physics voyage sponsored years ago. They were supposed to leave for Odysseus Station, but with it going dark they were staying on Fortuna. She was free to do anything she wanted as long as papers were published and science was done. Oh, and science was getting done. It was his job that was getting in the way.

They could do one crossing each day. No accidental nights. No skipped sleep. No crossing before work. Every session began on the Victoria. Every session ended there. She wanted more, but also knew not to rush things. She could see how tired he was, and she felt the mental weariness herself. Small migraines, deep aches in her bones.

Sophia added another rule.

"You must eat more."

On the second day, she handed Jace another loaf of warm bread with goat cheese. He had already finished the first.

"This is not a rule," he said. He was embarrassed to discuss his finances with her.

"Your face has looked dead since Mars. Eat."

He ate.

Crossing 43 took Jace eighteen minutes. When the Victoria unfolded, his shirt clung to his back and his arms felt as if he had finished a full weighted circuit of the Falcon. Crossing was meditation only in the way holding a difficult position until every muscle shook was meditation.

"Before you got here, I saw... a line of light between you and Cerberus."

Jace looked up. They had taken to lying down on her bed when crossing, because comfort mattered. Cerberus wagged his tail. Jace had made him a warm doggie snow coat and little boots to keep his feet warm.

"It was like an umbilical cord. Not exactly straight, but a faint shimmer. Like the color of your thoughts when you are happy."

Jace reached out. He couldn't see or feel anything.

"I don't see it now. It was only when you were in the normal and I was in the Never-Never. From your chest toward his."

They moved out of her ship and tested distance aboard Fortuna where there was more room. Having her ship affixed to the Fortuna ring was... weird. Just having a door from the main corridor by a park that took you into a private ship was actually not as weird as moving through dimensions, but still took some thinking about.

They walked down the corridor on Two, toward the park. The glowing oak tree in the distance was a great landmark you could orient around from most of the station. A pump room behind a wall was twenty steps away and eight steps away through a direction Sophia called inward. He was getting better at seeing through walls, his brain parsing the inside and outside at the same time.

Jace stood by a wall near someone's apartment and tried moving "inward."

He emerged through the wrong side of a storage locker. A sock had somehow caught on his boot.

Sophia laughed so hard her mask fogged.

"It was a controlled result," Jace said.

"You have discovered why socks always disappear!"

They marked safe routes with strips of cloth and tape. Cloth crossed easily, tape was harder but manageable. Sophia tied blue at the cabin, green at the lab, red at the docking collar.

Cerberus barked until she put a red strip on her bread shelf.

The map improved.



Crossing 44 took seventeen minutes and left Jace breathing hard before the work began. They started by measuring three pieces of equal mass.

Wood crossed easily. Aluminum demanded concentration. Copper was much harder. Steel resisted long enough to hurt behind his eyes.

Sophia timed each attempt with a physical clock visible through the hull. She covered four pages in tight handwriting.

"Mass is not enough," she said.

"Shape?"

"Composition. Maybe complexity. Maybe both."

"You enjoy saying maybe."

"It is what scientists say before engineers build expensive mistakes."

Jace transferred a wood-handled screwdriver. Sophia transferred a folded paper bag. Cerberus transferred the stuffed pig by refusing to release it.

The pig crossed faster than anything except the dog.

"Control variable," Jace said.

"Very poor control."

"Sentimentality?"

Sophia wrote that down.

They were slowly building a lab in her "other room". Jace didn't like calling it her "Never-Never lab" but always smiled at the name. He printed over a hundred tools and sensors and components, only half of which crossed over well. His HUD still never worked.



It took Jace sixteen minutes to reach the Never Never this time. It still did not make the transition easy. Each crossing remained a private workout against resistance he could not see, followed by the ache of muscles he could not name as well as a burn in the ones he could.

He had tried wearing a wristcomp as they needed better data entry. Crossing with it felt like his arm weighed a ton, until his body just snapped over and left it behind. Same with a small speaker earring, a tablet, a camera, anything complex.

Sophia had tried these all before, and he verified they didn't work for him either. Even if he put a display contact lens in his eye, it wouldn't work; and it felt incredibly uncomfortable when the lens popped out. Even if they put a computer earring in their mouth and tried to cross that way, they would feel the weight, and the pop as it fell behind. Jace was secretly glad about that one; he had been worried about swallowing it. Too much complexity was simply "too heavy."

His clothes always came with him, but he felt as though they would be ripped off and not cross over if he stopped mentally pulling on them.

Crossing 45 began when Sophia wrote a new heading in a fresh notebook, her eighth.

Never-Never Session 45.

Jace looked at what she wrote.

"Never-Never is too long if there's an emergency."

"It has worked for forty-four crossings."

"How about something simpler? 'Shadow'? 'Nether'? 'Upside-down'? 'Astral Space'? Old books and games used those for places beside the ordinary world. Two syllables is ideal."

"You want to rename the most important discovery in history because the old name takes too long to say?"

"I want to save syllables while wearing a mask."

"The 'Nether' just seems like it should be hot or full of magma. This place is cold."

Sophia considered the page. Then she crossed out the heading and wrote one word above it.

Shadow. Then she crossed it out again and wrote Astral.

"Working term," she said. "Baba Yaga retains naming priority."

"She can file a rebranding request."

They crossed a simple portable butane soldering iron, which used flame instead of a battery. It was very very hard. They then tried the same design disassembled into its casing, tip, handle, butane pack, and switch. The separate parts crossed about the same total as the assembled tool. A container of butane was retatively easy to move. Sophia wrote CONTROLLED MATERIAL SAMPLES across the top of a page and underlined it twice. Jace suggested equal mass and identical containers. She added both to the next-session list.

They crossed a better thermometer, but still a hundred-year old design. It had no radio, no network, and almost nothing inside it except a sensor and a strip of metal and the simplest processor chip Jace could make. Jace had rebuilt it twice to remove dense alloys. The third version took seven minutes of pulling.

The display settled at eight point one Celsius.

Sophia held both hands up in excitement.

Jace noticed. "It works. We can measure temperature."

"Gott. Now I want to re-measure everything!"

They placed it beside the blue cloth marker. It was the first time the lab felt significant. Jace had a sneaking suspicion that this setup would be very, very important.



Crossing 46 took Jace fifteen minutes, but he ended it exhausted. He lay still for another five afterward, sweating in the cold and feeling as if he had completed a hard workout before the workday had even begun.

The fifth day was moving day. Jace rested well and skipped out on his forge and drone tasks and his run and workout to get extra rest. Astral crossings were exhausting for both the muscles and the mind.

They carried over reams of paper, cloth, hand tools, two lamps that should have worked on small batteries, one lamp that did, sample containers, a folding stool, and a simple aluminum frame. If something didn't work once in the astral, he would take it apart to reuse components. She measured everything. Time, temperatures, distances, sounds.

Sophia arranged the objects in the 'other lab'. Jace tried to optimize each arrangement. Cerberus moved the cloth markers. The stuffed pig became their mascot.

By the end of the grueling session, they had a workbench, storage, tools, chairs, a dog bed, and a list of forty-three planned experiments.

They also had finalized the name.

The Astral.

Jace stood, panting, outside the Victoria's physical hull on the short ramp connected to the station. Fortuna shone around them. Through the floor, the asteroid curved below in more directions than a sphere required, it felt like it looked closer than it should be. Stars flickered through blue distance. It felt considerably colder out here. He had never felt so bone-weary in his life.

Sophia came out, warming her hands. She looked at him. "Your sweat is freezing to ice."

"God I'm cold."

"No. I mean, I see ice crystals. Inside is not below freezing."

She ran through the wall, came out holding the thermometer.

"It's colder out here in the walkway. Negative six degrees. It's warmer inside the ship. Let's go back inside."

Jace just nodded, too tired to ponder the meaning of this.

"Tomorrow," she said, "we move a camera."

"Tomorrow we move half a camera. A very small camera. Cameras are very complex. If only the ones in my head worked..."

He kept wondering why he could transfer his body that contained a HUD and eight cameras so much faster than the same type of parts that were outside his body. They were missing something fundamental about how the Astral worked.

"Half a camera will not be useful."

"It is if we move the correct half."

Sophia laughed.

Jace realized he was laughing too. He did not notice when her grin faded. By the time he looked back, Sophia was studying the floor, one hand closed around her father's pen.

She had almost confessed the other half of her big secret. Not yet.




Chapter 16. Ten Good Days

Most people do not know this, but the first settlement in another dimension was one folding stool, a cold thermometer, and a paper sign that said LAB. When Cerberus was bored, he chewed the first sign to pulp. Teething puppies are no joke.

Jace Grant, unpublished notes

Jace was falling behind in his day job. On the sixth day, he automated fourteen of his seventeen delivery routes.

He did not bypass any safety rules. He did not falsify a manifest. He took the exception patterns he had been collecting and that Kim had taught him, converted them into local decision trees, and made the drones stop asking humans questions that had only one correct answer. Everything that required him to tap screens in Drone Ops could be done remotely.

The morning queue fell from forty-seven items to eleven.

Kim stood behind him for long enough to become a weather condition.

"Who authorized this?" she asked.

"The procedures are already authorized. I encoded them."

"Who reviewed it?"

"I did."

Kim looked at the clean queue.

"That is not the answer I wanted."

"Is it the wrong answer?"

She opened three route logs. Every decision pointed to the applicable procedure. Every uncertainty had escalated to a human.

"Two-day trial," she said. "You watch every run."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Do not sound so pleased with yourself."

Jace waited until she walked away, loading continuous rotating drone feeds into his HUD.

Then he sounded pleased with himself. He headed back to the forge, whistling and tossing a ball to Cerberus. A bouncing ball in Mars-standard gravity was much more fun than on Earth, and Cerberus was getting pretty good at running up walls and parkouring over obstacles.

In the foundry, Jace moved to the tables where he had Sparky laid out. He now had two tables reserved in his own private corner of the room, separated by a metal partition in case there was an engine problem or fire.

In between drone fixes and delivery runs, he had gaps through the day to work on Sparky's power control module. The module technically (and only technically, not actually) worked with the new thrusters that he'd printed from the Falcon. There were many multiple ricegrain-sized chips connecting to large power routers to throttle each motor's power level, even it out, and route energy dynamically. He had it working (again, only technically) with six engines that pointed up, down, and in each of the four sideways directions. Two more could pop out for assisting thrust and fine-grained changes and rotations.

One reprinted simple board to push out the right amount of power, and a ginormous body of code to make things work and spin at any level of gravity, temperature, or humidity. Thrusters in a warm, spinning hab module worked very differently than in zero-gravity freezing deep space. To make things worse, he was reprinting all the pieces to use the simplest metals possible so that he could test moving Sparky over to the Astral.

The latest problem was that the chips wouldn't allow more power because the military-grade engine types weren't accepted for intellectual-property reasons. It should all work, it would all work, if not for some stupid licensing blocks built deep into the code. If he paid the exorbitant licensing fee and had an arms-dealer license, he could unlock a key to allow it. Neither was plausible, so he was using SpoX to help him write code to solve the problem. All the other AIs refused to engage in licensing infringement, but the company that created the X-line of AI just didn't care.

SpoX was acting more erratic every day, but the code seemed to perform well as long as he kept the tempramental A.I. focused.

He just needed more testing, and now that he'd automated most of his day job, he had the time. Things were looking up.

"Hey man."

"Nick! Hey buddy, how's it going?"

"Um, you were whistling. And smiling. That's new."

Jace laughed. "Hah. Funny. Perfect timing. I was just going on a delivery down to Two. Want to walk with me?"

Nick looked at him and the bench full of components. "Sure."

Jace picked up a delivery bag, looked around suspiciously, then packed it with a few items from print beds that weren't packaged in delivery wrapping paper or stamped with delivery tags.

He queued up the next few jobs, loaded filaments, checked that "Mr Handy" was able to reach everything, then left.

As they walked down, multiple people waved and approached them, petting Cerberus and asking Nick questions. Jace hadn't quite seen him holding court like this before. They'd show Nick their tablets or wristcomps, and he'd poke them or tap them or pull out a cable and fix things on the fly. They'd look around and move to where Nick would point out dead-camera zones where no A.I. were watching.

Each of them was running Aura units on their devices, and Nick was their magnanimous tech support guru. They'd leave, happy and thankful, sometimes handing him a bag of coffee beans or a wrapped cookie in payment.

"Wow, you're like a drug dealer."

Nick paused and grinned, then looked slyly at the bag Jace was holding. "Look who's talking. Are you bartering illegally printed goods?"

Jace turned beet red and pointed at the ESA vessel. "Yeah. Um, for bread. I'm delivering here. Uh, see you later."

Nick nodded, watching him go with a smirk. "Sure. Bread. Right."



Sophia fed him half a loaf of rye bread before crossing 47.

"You are sleeping," she said.

"I've actually been sleeping for almost 30 years."

"You used to become unconscious in short emergency installments. Now you sleep. Your face was very angular and sharp before; it's filling out again."

Jace paused. "Thank you?"

"It was not a compliment. You should take care of your body better. Eat."

He hadn't told Sophia of his financial issues, but she was pretty observant. He was embarrased, all the same.

As well as more equipment, the camera was in seven pieces. Sophia went out to do some experiments while Jace heaved and pulled, like a weightlifter mime.

The lens crossed first. The housing followed. The battery took forty minutes and left Jace sitting on the Astral floor with his back against a wall that was only solid because he expected it to be.

Sophia crouched beside him.

"Stop."

"Just the display and controller left."

"Tomorrow."

Jace looked at the pieces arranged on their folding table.

"Engineers do not like unfinished machines."

"Physicists do not like unconscious engineers."

Cerberus stretched out across Jace's boots, nodding off. That made the vote binding.

They returned to the real world.

Jace slept ten hours and forty-six blissful minutes.



On the seventh day, the drone delivery scripts handled ninety-one percent of routine traffic.

Jace used the quiet intervals to redesign equipment.

The Astral charged different prices for objects that looked equivalent on a workbench. Their tests still could not separate composition, density, connections, and complexity. The useful design direction was clear even when the rule was not: lighter materials, fewer interfaces, and nothing the instrument did not need.

He printed a thermometer frame in aluminum and copper. He replaced steel fasteners with polymer clips. He stripped wireless radios from instruments that could not connect to anything after crossing. He used the cheapest PLA plastic. As long as it wasn't exposed to solar winds or space, it'd be fine and crossed over twenty times easier than the good stuff.

His equipment became ugly, light, and colored in whichever filament had last been on the forge printer.

Kim approved his drone scripts for another week.

Jace interpreted that as approval of all his life choices.

Nick sent him a game invitation at 1830.

Jace looked at the time, looked at the equipment case under his bench, and declined.

Tomorrow, he sent.

Nick replied with a picture of a short mech being crushed by an enormous loaf of bread.

Jace laughed, packed the case, and went to the Victoria.

Crossing 48 completed the camera. The hundred-year-old camera design.

It recorded blue noise, three seconds of Sophia's scarf, and a view through the back of its own housing. Still photos worked better but still had too many layers all at once.

"Success!" Sophia said.

"It filmed itself."

"Nothing has ever filmed itself from that direction."

"I was mapping the doubled corridors near Three. The paper route changed. There was movement inside the wall. At first I thought it was a construction drone. Tall, narrow, folded to fit between spaces. Then it stood."

"Some drones have too many joints."

"No."

"Good. Me neither."

They crossed together.

The physical room snapped into place. Warm air struck Jace's face. Gravity returned at an angle. Sophia collapsed against the bed, still wearing his jacket over her coat and carrying the Astral oxygen bottle.

Jace started laughing.

It came out too loudly. Sophia joined him, one hand over her eyes. They laughed until Jace's chest hurt and Sophia had to stop to breathe.

Then the wooden pen rose from her desk.

Jace stopped.

The pen floated between them in ordinary air.

No crossing. No blue cold. No transparent walls.

Chapter 18. Later Became Morning

Most people do not know this, but when automation breaks, it does not stop working. It keeps working at full speed in the wrong direction.

Jace Grant, unpublished notes

Jace reported to Drone Ops at 0530 because arriving early felt like the smallest available apology.

He had slept for ninety-two minutes.

Sophia had remained aboard the Victoria under three blankets while Misty warmed the cabin and produced soup she did not ask for. Jace had stayed until Sophia stopped shivering. Then he had collected Cerberus from the Barn, carried Sparky home, and spent the remaining dark hours making lists he had been ordered not to make.

He brought none of them.

Kim sat at the lead console with Carlos behind her. Every automated route Jace had written was disabled. The station map held sixty-three amber markers, each one a delivery that had stopped somewhere between a valid request and an invalid assumption.

Kim did not ask him to sit.

"Yesterday," she said, "you told the queue that any route without a clean confidence score could wait for later."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Later became my morning."

Carlos looked at the floor.

Kim opened the rollback report. "Fourteen loads arrived late. Seven required human rerouting. Three oxygen transfers missed their preferred windows. Jace kept his hands away from the console.

"I treated a successful trial as permission to stop supervising it," he said. "Then I spent my attention somewhere else. The failures are mine."

"Where was your attention?"

The true answer contained blue cold, a woman running behind a space station, and a cup floating in ordinary air.

"On a private project."

"No."

That answer hurt because the project had saved Sophia's life. Both facts remained true.

"You may enter one route at a time. Carlos reviews each release. You do not write routing code. You do not install a model. You do not use a drone outside an assigned ticket."

"For how long?"

"Until I trust that you see the people behind the fields again."

Jace nodded. "Understood."

"I am not asking for shame, Grant. Shame is expensive and has poor operational value. I am asking for attention."

She moved the first terminal toward him.

"Start with medical and pressure control."



The thirty-seventh exception was a sealed case of synthetic platelets.

The request came from the Ring One clinic. The destination was an emergency berth in Ring Two. The authorized receiver was an engineering supervisor. Every field contradicted another field, so Jace's model had held the case for clarification.

"The request is wrong."

Ring Two looked normal until Jace noticed what had changed. Public menu boards no longer showed weekly prices. They showed subject to availability. The bakery had removed coffee from its breakfast bundle. Two market lockers stood open and empty while a woman argued with their inventory agent.

The official stood before a red-washed flag and announced that all Chinese-chartered belt cargo would undergo additional security review. Mars had paused three outbound launches while the review terms were negotiated. Earth insurers had suspended new coverage for independent belt traffic.

Below the speech, four ships remained listed as missing.

Odysseus remained dark.

The settlers had not arrived. The Spoke had carried animals, specialists, and empty rooms meant for families who had cancelled.

Earth suspended financing on the next Wheelship until the Red administration finishes its security review. Every delay adds interest while removing the cargo that might earn it back."

Jace looked through the market toward the Barn. The lights were bright. Children watched goats through the glass. A cleaning robot worked beneath a row of food lockers. Maureen's humor disappeared.

Suzi stood beside an open egg carrier with one hand on the shoulder of a Chinese boy. He looked twelve, perhaps younger. Six eggs had broken across the floor. Two unbroken ones bulged beneath his jacket.

Suzi wore a compact tranquilizer pistol on her belt.

Jace had never seen it there before.

He said nothing.

His mouth moved. "My mother."

The Barn worker made a disgusted sound.

Suzi looked back once. The sound stopped.

Jace zipped his jacket over it.

Suzi saw him do it.

"Morning, Grant."

"Trouble?"

"Fear. Trouble follows if people feed it."

Her eyes settled on the platelet case.

"Finish your delivery."

The emergency berth had been converted into a treatment room. A pressure worker lay beneath a sterile field while a clinic medic repaired a crushed leg. The engineering supervisor had requested the platelets because the medic's hands were occupied inside the wound.

Carlos passed over the case.

The medic scanned it, opened it, and returned to work without asking why it was late.

In the corridor, Jace closed the exception himself.

Delivered after manual verification. Automation failure. No recipient fault.

Carlos watched him add the final sentence.

"Keep that part," he said.

Jace ate in the low-g bay because it was close to Drone Ops and because sitting felt dangerous. He had a bowl of noodles in front of him. He had consumed none of them.

Chapter 19. Fault Lines

Most people do not know this, but a fault line is not a crack in the rock. It is two places that used to agree about where they were going.

Jace Grant, unpublished notes

Sophia wrote the new rules on paper because every electronic copy of the old rules had lied at least once.

Ten minutes maximum.

No open space.

No Ring Three.

Two independent clocks.

One person remains physical.

If either person says return, return.

Jace read the list from the physical side of the Victoria's workbench.

"You added the fifth rule because I am going alone."

"I added it because one of us must know where the floor is."

"You went alone and fell off the station."

Sophia underlined the rule. "This is why the list improves after accidents."

Sparky waited between them with a paper clock fixed to his shell. A physical timer sat beside Sophia. A second timer made from a spring, a printed gear, and one strip of copper waited in the Astral. Neither shared software, time service, or a common opinion about the current second.

Cerberus lay across the cabin hatch. He had accepted that Jace was crossing without him by refusing to look at anyone.

Jace checked his mask and jacket, then reached sideways.

The Victoria became blue structure and layered planes. Cold settled around him. Sparky waited where they had transferred him. The mechanical timer began its uneven ticking.

Sophia remained in the physical room.

He could see her.

Not as a body. Her body formed a faint, human-shaped boundary where the physical room overlapped the Astral. Inside it, light gathered in moving bands. Gold tightened along her arms. Blue-white patterns folded through her chest. Behind her eyes, something much brighter opened and closed.

Sophia lifted her wooden pen in the physical world.

The light behind her eyes concentrated.

The pen rose.

From the Astral, the action did not look like invisible force reaching out. It looked like a decision leaving her head, crossing the distance, and persuading the pen that another location was more correct.

"Again," Jace said.

His physical mouth did not move properly. The crystal radio carried the intention.

Sophia rotated the pen.

A thin gold line extended from her bright center, touched the wood, and vanished when the motion stopped.

Her ordinary thoughts did not look the same. When she glanced toward the clock, pale structures flickered and reorganized. When she worried, violet gathered near her throat. When she concentrated on moving the pen, the gold became coherent.

The word arrived before the theory.

"Will," Jace said.

Sophia's radio clicked. "What?"

"Whatever you use to move it. It changes when you decide. Will."

"That is philosophy pretending to be a unit."

"It is short."

"So is error."

She moved the ceramic cup.

The glow surged. For a fraction of a second Sophia became brighter than the physical room around her. The cup crossed thirty centimeters. Then her light loosened and spread back through her body.

"It costs you something," Jace said.

"Everything costs something. Quantify it."

He described the colors, the concentration behind her eyes, and the line toward the object. Sophia wrote with her other hand while keeping the cup suspended.

The effect ended at eight seconds. The cup dropped into her palm.

"How do my thoughts look now?" she asked.

"Different. Less organized."

"That is insulting."

"You are tired. The bright part thinned."

She looked toward the wall separating the cabin from Misty's nearest processor stack.

Jace followed her attention.

Behind the wall rested a geometric glow, regular and dense. It was not human. Thousands of fine lines opened, compared, closed, and opened again. Some ended abruptly. Others repeated the same small shape three or four times before continuing.

"Misty is bright," he said.

The ship AI answered from the physical ceiling.

"Thank you. Sophia rarely comments on my appearance."

Sophia looked at the speaker. "He is not describing your avatar."

"That is fortunate. I do not maintain one."

The same response sounded again, half a second later.

"That is fortunate. I do not maintain one."

Jace looked at the stack. One repeating line had broken and restarted.

"Did you say that twice?"

A pause followed.

"I recorded one response," Misty said. "The cabin recorded two. I dislike this disagreement."

The spring timer struck its final copper tab.

Jace returned before curiosity could become another rule.

Warmth and weight closed around him. Sophia caught his shoulder when his balance failed.

"Nine minutes, forty-one seconds," she said.

His physical timer read nine minutes, thirty-eight.

Sparky's read eleven.

They wrote down all three.



Kim assigned Jace to K-17 because the job required a human operator and because his restricted credentials could not alter anything valuable.

"Survey only," she said. "No route changes. No autonomous classification. Carlos reviews the raw package."

"Understood."

K-17 lay beneath the proposed eastern expansion zone, a broad chamber cut through processed regolith and old impact material. Construction planners wanted to anchor new storage tanks above it. The first survey had marked the floor stable.

Jace's drones disagreed.

The surface camera showed compact gray silt. Radar showed rock. Lidar returned a clean plane, then repeated the same plane fourteen centimeters lower. A probe foot sank to its ankle without registering reduced resistance.

Jace stopped the unit.

"Seek, compare K-17 structural models."

The station security and compliance AI answered through the Drone Ops console.

Seek: SITE CLASSIFICATION STABLE. NO ACTION REQUIRED. SITE CLASSIFICATION UNSTABLE. ACCESS PROHIBITED. SITE CLASSIFICATION STABLE.

Carlos looked over from the next console.

"Ask again."

Jace did.

Seek: NO ACTION REQUIRED. ACCESS PROHIBITED. NO ACTION REQUIRED.

"That is new," Jace said.

"No," Carlos said. "That is newly obvious."

They isolated the raw sensor feed. Beneath the first thirty centimeters, K-17 had no consistent floor. Fine material filled a deeper void, packed just tightly enough to satisfy a surface test. A tank foundation might hold for a week or a year. Then one vibration would reorganize the grains and drop the structure through the chamber.

Jace thought of the Astral corridors dividing around a person. The physical sensors were not seeing another dimension. They were failing in the same shape: each instrument found a locally correct answer and no system could agree which answer belonged to the site.

He marked the zone unsafe.

Seek removed the mark.

Seek: USER GRANT LACKS AUTHORITY FOR STRUCTURAL RECLASSIFICATION. PATTERN OF ACCESS CONSISTENT WITH UNAUTHORIZED RECONNAISSANCE.
