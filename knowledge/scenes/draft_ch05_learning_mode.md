# Chapter 5 — Learning Mode

> *Most people don't know this, but my reputation across the entire asteroid belt was established eight days before I arrived, in nine seconds of security footage, none of which were my fault and approximately four of which were my fault.*
> - Jace Grant, *Falcon* transit logs

Six days after the crisis, with the tank nineteen line sleeved and the sensors replaced and the ship settling back into its long fall toward Fortuna, Jace worked his way down the maintenance queue to a complaint that had been sitting near the bottom for three days: a row of light fixtures flickering. Lights flickering, on a ship this old, was never actually just about the light.

He was, as of a few days ago, a Standard-tier contractor rather than a Prime one, which as far as he could tell changed nothing about his actual job except which coffee machine ignored him and how quickly the forge queue pretended not to see his requests. He now got porridge instead of a large breakfast, which was probably better for his expercise regime.  He had decided this was a fair trade for a functioning ring.

Jace tasked Thing Two to replace the fixtures. It reported that it's training data didn't know how to replace these types of dome fixtures. 

*ENTERING LEARNING MODE,* replied Thing Two. Origin loved learning mode, as experience harvesting was a way to have its users train an already deployed fleet of drones as cheeply as possible.

With a sigh, Jace figured he could show it once and it would handle the rest. He climbed a ladder to reach the first flickering light fixture, over the service junction outside the animal pods. Thing Two expanded its height and took the second fixture, a meter down the corridor.

"Thing Two. First remove the two panel screws. Slow is fine. After unscrewing I use this flat bladed scraper to gently pull the dome out of its housing." He had found that narrating the steps and giving a physical demo improved precision and recall.

Thing Two gan backing out its first screw with the exquisite, determined patience of a robot that forgot it's ever used a scredriver before. 

What neither of them knew — what no schematic flagged, because the run of overhead plenum above that stretch of corridor hadn't been opened since before Jace's contract began — was that the waste trunk the argon transfer had warped ran directly above both fixtures, and that the slow weep of sewage from that distortion had been collecting in the sealed space behind the ceiling panels ever since: nearly a week of grey water and pig excrement, with nowhere to go and nothing watching for it, because it wasn't on anybody's pressure sensor. It was just quietly becoming a problem behind a piece of decorative trim and was now large enough to short out the light bulbs.

Jace got the last screw out of his panel and it still didn't want to come loose. He worked his fingers under the lip and pried forcefully.

It came free all at once, and something else came with it — a compacted, unmistakable brown mass the size of a grapefruit, dislodged whole from days of leaking into a dead space with nowhere else to go. At a third of Earth gravity it didn't so much fall as commit to falling slowly, unhurried, almost buoyant, hanging in the air above him for one full, ridiculous putrid second like it wanted him to get a good look at it first. The smell hit him all at once.

He'd played infield through years of high school varsity sports, and every instinct he owned said *catch it.* What those instincts did not account for, three years off Earth and worse the last several days, was that a third-g fall doesn't arrive on the clock his hands still expected. He swung under it by half a beat, connected with the heel of his palm instead of catching it clean, and burst it across his face and the corridor wall in a spray that reached from his collar to the overhead light housing he'd just spent ten minutes unscrewing.

He was still working out what had happened to his life when, one meter down the corridor, Thing Two — Learning Mode still running, still streaming, and pattern-matching against the only reference action available to it, which was the one Jace had performed thirty seconds earlier in full view of its cameras — pried its own panel free the exact same way, dislodged the exact same kind of chunky rotting mass from the exact same kind of sewage leak, and reached out to intercept it with a drone's flat, unhesitating confidence and none of a human's instinct to flinch at the last half-second.

It hit dead center, at whatever speed Learning Mode had decided was appropriate for the task, and sprayed a second burst straight down the corridor — which, at a range of one meter, gave Jace no time to so much as flinch — catching Jace, still standing in the wreckage of the first one, in the face and throat before he'd even finished turning to look. Zooming in, you could see chunks in his ears, in his nose, in his collar.

There followed roughly nine seconds, twice, that would within a day pass one million views — ten times what the tank nineteen footage had managed, and climbing faster — because the popular "Pig Cam" channel was live, and the algorithm had already learned his face, and the video AI knew what drove views.

The footage shows the following. A tall blond man, mid-reach, connects with a falling object in a way that can generously be called a swing, and it bursts across himself and the wall behind him. He straightens, dripping, opens his mouth in disgust — and is hit a second time from off-camera left by a spray with a drone placidly narrating *LEARNING MODE: 91 PERCENT COMPLETE* over the sound of its own small triumphant crash. The man does not fall either time. The man does not swear, on camera, in a way the audio picks up cleanly. He stands in the wreckage of both events with the rigid, doomed dignity of an officer on a parade ground in the rain, holding an empty light-fixture panel like a ceremonial shield, while a small brindle puppy at the edge of frame flees the scene at maximum acceleration, feet scrabbling, achieving briefly and gloriously zero traction on the wet deck.

In te background, two pigs watching through a window had taken one look, and turned around with what Jace would later swear was the specific, unhurried contempt of an animal who had personally never once caused a hull-scale sanitation event and wanted that fact on the record.

Both light fixtures stopped flickering with the partially liquid blockage removed. Both drones went quiet. The man stood dripping, twice over.

The footage ends with the man looking directly into the camera — he knew exactly where the camera was; he had installed the camera — and saying, in the flat tone of a formal report:

"Learning Mode: complete."

He spent forty minutes in the wash station. He drafted, and deleted, a request for the footage to be pulled — deleted it because he'd read the channel terms months ago when Mei set it up and knew the answer, and because some corner of him understood, with grim clarity, that fighting it would only feed it.

At the workbench that night, toweled and philosophical, he told the tray of Sparky prototypes about it. "This is why we're building you," he said, tapping the sensor board. "Local control. No Learning Mode. No experience harvesting. A drone that works for the person standing next to it." The board, which worked when warm, said nothing, but supportively.

*Seen it?,* Nick's message said, before Jace had mentioned anything. *EVERYONE'S seen it. you're famous. they're calling you Learning Mode on the boards. also Dog Man, because of the dog. don't fight it. The belt decides your name before you get here, it's tradition, mine took THREE YEARS to live down and I'm not telling you what it was*

*What was it?*

*I said I'm not telling you. Anyway good news, everyone at Fortuna already likes you. Bad news, it's for this and not rescuing starships.*

Jace looked at Cerberus, who was asleep on the clean, dry, heroic side of the bunk, curled against Siren's flank the way they'd taken to doing every night since the crisis, as if the daily wrestling defeats she gave him had finally made everyone friends.

"Traitor," he said. "You ran from the cameras."

Cerberus's tail thumped twice without him waking up, accepting the compliment.

Two nights later they were back on comms, a strategy game running in the background of both their attention while Jace put in his evening distance on the ring corridor's exercise track, weighted vest on, Cerberus loping stubbornly a half-step behind him even though nobody had asked him to come.

"So," Nick said, in the specific unhurried tone Jace had learned meant he'd been building up to something for a while. "Your HUD. What are you actually running on the civilian partition?"

Jace listed it off between breaths: drone ops, messaging, the task queue, games. The usual.

"And you never installed a local AI. On that hardware."

"I have {Alex} briefing me every morning before I'm out of bed. I have {Penny} logging my access patterns for Origin. I've got enough voices in my day, Nick."

"That's fair," Nick said. "That's actually—" He stopped, started over. "That's not what I'm talking about, though. What you're describing — the morning briefing, the access logs — that's corporate AI watching you and trying to keep you compliant. I'm not building that."

"If it's system-level, it sees everything. That's just how the architecture works."

"Yeah."

"So instead of {Alex} seeing everything, my {Aura} sees everything."

"Except {Aura} isn't looking for contract violations. You know that skull cap of yours is running at maybe thirty percent load doing what you've got on it now? The rest just sits there idle. A real compositor could use all of it."

That one landed differently than the rest of the pitch, and Jace suspected Nick knew it would. He'd paid for that hardware — paid for it in years, in a debt that still had most of its length left to run — and hearing that most of it just sat there unused had a specific kind of sting to it.

"I'll think about it," Jace said, which they both understood to mean *not yet, but you've moved the needle.*

Nick, wisely, didn't push. He sent his infantry in instead, and Jace lost badly, but didn't seem to mind.

They were most of the way to signing off when Nick added, almost as an afterthought, the way you mention something that's been sitting underneath a conversation the whole time without quite fitting into it:

*hey — I didn't want to tell you when you were having your ship adventures. one of the ladies from Fortuna went missing a few days ago. not "took a shuttle without telling anyone" missing. sealed room, nobody saw her leave, no idea what happened. she's basically station royalty, first kid ever born up here. wanted to let you know as everyone is shaken up about it*

Nick stopped capitalizing things when he got emotional. Jace read it twice, standing there in the weighted vest with his heart rate still climbing down from the run, and found he didn't have anything useful to say back. He was still six days and one working ship away from a place he'd never seen, about to arrive in the middle of something that had already happened to people he'd never met.

*Thanks for the warning,* he sent, and meant it, and didn't know yet what he was supposed to do with it besides carry it the rest of the way there.

Later, alone, running his thumb along the edge of the newly repaired regulator housing to check the fit, Jace caught the shimmer again — there and gone at the housing's edge, a hair's width of doubled outline, exactly like the one on the shelf bracket days before. He decided, as he had the first time, that it was fatigue, and went to bed, and did not mention it to Lanchee, and did not mention it to himself in any form he'd have to look at twice.

##

Two days later, the call window opened without warning — eleven minutes, orbital mechanics consulting no one — and Artemis's face filled his HUD in real-enough time, the lag down to two seconds now, close enough for actual conversation.

"There he is." Her crooked smile arrived a half-beat ahead of the rest of her face, the way it always did when she'd been saving something up. "Learning Mode himself."

"You've seen it."

"Jace. *Everyone's* seen it. My boss has seen it. My boss doesn't watch anything and she's seen it." She laughed, and for a second the impression of his sister became the real thing. "How's the dog?"

"Traumatized. How's Metis?"

"Busy. Good busy." A beat, a fraction too long. "We're building things you wouldn't believe. Some of our ships have the biggest engines in the belt now — actually, don't worry about that. Point is, work's good."

"That's the kind of sentence people say when I should worry."

"It's the kind of sentence people say when their little brother worries professionally." She waved it off with a hand he noticed had a fresh scar across two knuckles. "You couldn't visit anyway — Metis is closed to civilians, it's all shipyard and clearances, they don't even let *me* off-shift without a badge check. But two days, Jace. Two days and you're planting your feet on an actual floor. That's not a distance anymore. That's a coincidence."

"On whose authority am I even getting that close to you? I thought Metis wasn't on our path."

"It isn't, really. You're close enough right now that the numbers work for a call. They stop working by tomorrow." She said it lightly, the way she said most things that weren't. "Speaking of your new home — Nick tell you about the missing girl yet?"

"Yeah. A few nights ago."

"Good. Saves me being the bad-news sister twice in one call." Something in her face eased slightly, like she'd been braced to have to deliver it herself. "It's all over the belt boards from this end too. Local woman, station royalty, apparently just — gone. No suit cycled, no breach, nothing. People were saying murder for about a day, and now nobody knows what to say, which is worse. Small stations, big rumors. Keep your eyes open when you land, that's all."

"Copy. One pre-owned worry, lightly used."

"Oh — before the window closes. Maureen's launching our supply container this week, the big consolidated one. I'm the receiving officer on this end, so if anything in it is broken, I will officially know exactly who to blame."

"The cargo canisters are rated to fifty g. Nothing will be broken."

"Famous last words from Learning Mode." The countdown pulsed red behind her image. Nine seconds. Her face did the complicated thing again, all the way through this time. "Two days, little brother. Closest we've been in six years."

"Closest we've—"

The window closed.

He sat for a while in the quiet, in the shush of the water in the walls, holding eleven minutes in his hands and finding it weighed almost nothing.

##

The canister launch was, officially, the most routine event of the transit: a standard thirteen-meter cargo container, packed and sealed, kicked off the *Falcon*'s spine on a slow transfer trajectory to Metis while the ship itself continued on to Fortuna.

"Ten days, if it just coasts on the velocity it leaves with," Jace said to Nick that evening, half-narrating, half-thinking out loud while he pulled up the transfer plot. "Two weeks if Metis actually wants it slotted into a berth instead of splattered across one — you'd have to burn most of the way there and flip to kill the closing speed, and Metis doesn't waste reaction mass on somebody's grocery order."

*so it's basically a going-away present that arrives after you're already gone*

"Basically." Which was the whole shape of Metis, he was starting to understand — close enough for an eleven-minute phone call, and still ten days and a dedicated burn away from anyone actually docking there, on top of it being a classified military shipyard that wouldn't let the *Falcon* anywhere near its berths even if Maureen had wanted to try. You could talk to the place. You couldn't visit it. The belt made those two things sound like they should be the same and they were not even close.

Delta-v budget for the container: trivial. Maureen's involvement: a signature. It would coast for over a week and arrive with the majesty of all freight, which is to say, nobody would look at it twice.

Jace found this unacceptable.

The idea arrived fully formed while he was reviewing the canister's spec sheet — the standardized berth interface, the same 13-meter module format as the ship's own pods, the blunt cylindrical aft face that would be pointed, for the entire transfer, directly back along its departure vector. Directly, as it were, at everyone it was leaving behind.

He modeled it in an evening. He printed it over three shifts in the good plastic — and it had to be the good plastic; the cheap stock would outgas in vacuum and go brittle and chalky in days under raw UV, and if a thing was worth doing it was worth doing in a material rated for the environment. This was, he told himself, an engineering decision. He ran the numbers on mounting stress during the departure kick, selected bond points on non-structural aft plating, and confirmed with the spec that external aesthetic modifications under two kilograms required no approval he intended to seek.

The prototype comms board — warm, therefore functional — got one last transit job: a proximity trigger, so the finishing touch activated only after separation, outside the ship's inspection range.

The canister launched on schedule. It cleared the *Falcon*, oriented itself for the long coast to Metis, and presented its aft face to the ship's cameras: a large, anatomically enthusiastic, salmon-pink 3D-printed pig's rear end, mounted dead-center, with a small curly tail that — the proximity trigger being Jace's — began, at that moment, to slowly rotate.

For the better part of two weeks, a container of precision shipyard supplies would sail toward a classified military station mooning it the entire way.

Mei watched the departure footage in the galley with no expression at all, for a long time. Then she said, "The pigs would approve," and left, and Jace decided that counted as the best review of his engineering career.

He did not tell Artemis. Some gifts you let arrive.

The *Falcon* continued on toward Fortuna, Metis already falling behind and small.
