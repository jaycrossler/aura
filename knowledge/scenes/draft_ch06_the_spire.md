# Chapter 6. The Spire

> *Most people don't know this, but a spaceship's quietest places are the ones closest to its spine. Less spin, less gravity, less noise. I used to think that meant less of everything. I was wrong about that in a way I didn't enjoy.*
> — Jace Grant, *Falcon* transit logs

<!-- beat:t06_spire.beat1_dream_and_waking -->
Two nights after the canister launch, two days from Fortuna now, close enough that Carlos's name had started appearing in Jace's onboarding paperwork — the dream was the same shape it had been for well over a week now: a lattice of light running through the ship like veins through a leaf, every strut and conduit traced in something that wasn't quite color, warm and slow and enormous — except this time there was a place in it that was *looking back*. Not a shape. A presence, gathered up close along the ship's spine like something curled around a heat source, patient in a way that had nothing human in it and dripped with hostility. He couldn't have said how he knew both of those things. He just did, the way you know a room is occupied before you've located the person in it.

He woke sitting halfway up, heart going, certain — not frightened-certain, worse than that, *sure*-certain, the way you're sure of a smell — that he had actually seen it. Not dreamed it. Seen it, through however many decks of hull and deck plate stood between his bunk and the ship's central axis, the same way he'd been able to see, for about four days now, the shape of the water main behind his workshop wall in the half-second before his eyes caught up and told him it was just a wall.

His head didn't hurt. It hadn't hurt in well over a week. He'd stopped waiting for it to come back.

Cerberus was already awake, sitting bolt upright at the foot of the bunk, ears flat, staring at the door like it had said something.

"Yeah," Jace said. "I know."

He didn't remember deciding to get up. He remembered his hand closing around the first tool in reach on the workbench on his way past — a heavy adjustable wrench, cold, familiar, entirely the wrong tool for whatever this was, and he took it anyway because it was there and because his hands wanted something in them.

<!-- beat:t06_spire.beat2_the_climb -->
The central spire ran the long axis of the *Falcon*, the one place aboard where spin gravity thinned out toward nothing the higher you climbed. Nobody went up there. There was nothing to do up there — no bunks, no crops, no animals, just conduit runs and the reaction control cluster and a maintenance ladder nobody had used since the last drydock. Jace had walked past the access hatch a hundred times without once wondering what was actually behind it.

Cerberus made it three rungs up the ladder before his back legs found no purchase at all in the thinning gravity, scrabbled, and gave up. He dropped back to the deck and stayed there, barking — not at Jace, not at anything Jace could see, just a flat, steady, worried sound aimed up the shaft that didn't stop the whole time Jace climbed, and somehow got worse the higher he went, until it was more felt than heard, a pressure keeping time with his pulse.

The gravity thinned with every rung. By the top third of the climb it had dropped enough that his own weight stopped feeling reliable, and the dream-lattice feeling came back with it — strong enough now that he couldn't tell anymore where the vertigo of near-zero-g ended and the other thing started. The bulkheads seemed, for half-seconds at a time, to have a second surface just behind the first one, glowing faintly, gone the instant he tried to look straight at it. He told himself it was blood pressure. He told himself it a second time, louder, and kept climbing.

<!-- beat:t06_spire.beat3_the_panel -->
The access panel he stopped at wasn't anything special — an unlabeled maintenance plate over what his HUD, when he finally thought to check, insisted was an empty run of unused conduit. Structurally inert. Nothing behind it worth a panel at all.

It was also, unmistakably, the source of the feeling. The warmth. The looking-back.

He pulled up the diagnostic overlay one more time, hovering his hand an inch off the panel. HUD: empty cavity, ambient temperature nominal, no anomalies logged. His own eyes, in the half-second before the overlay caught up and smoothed the image over: something in there. Small. More than one something. A soft, wrong, patient glow that had no business existing in a sealed conduit run on a hard-vacuum ship.

He got the wrench under the panel's lip and worked it loose, screw by screw, hands steadier than they had any right to be.

The panel came away.

Two eggs sat nested in the dead conduit space, leathery, faintly luminous, each about the size of his fist. He had perhaps a second and a half to register that this made no physical sense at all before both of them split open at once.

<!-- beat:t06_spire.beat4_the_fight -->
What came out of them moved like nothing he'd ever fought — low, fast, wrong-angled, more joints than the space should have allowed, and it hit him before he'd finished flinching back from the panel. Claws found his forearm and opened it to the bone through his sleeve. He didn't feel it yet. He got the wrench up between himself and the second one on pure reflex and it deflected off something that gave like cartilage and rang like metal at the same time.

There was no room to think in the narrow maintenance space, only room to move — a shoulder into the bulkhead, a boot that found nothing to brace against in the thin gravity and sent him tumbling half a meter sideways into the second creature's reach, the wrench coming around in a wild overhand arc that connected with the first one's head with everything his whole body had in the swing.

It didn't so much die as *end* — burst apart into a silent, silver-white flare of light that filled the whole maintenance space for less than a second and then simply wasn't there anymore, and something in that half-second passed through him, cool and quick, gone before he had a name for the sensation, already forgotten under the more urgent problem of the second creature launching itself past his shoulder, straight at the bulkhead behind him, and disappearing directly through solid metal like the wall wasn't there. No hole. No breach alarm. No hull warning. It was simply, impossibly, gone, the way a held breath is gone once you let it out.

Jace hung in the near-zero-g dark of the spire, one arm bleeding freely into his sleeve, wrench still raised, breathing like he'd run a marathon, staring at an empty conduit space that no longer held anything at all.

<!-- beat:t06_spire.beat5_alex_and_institutional_blindness -->
He reported it. He didn't know why he reported it — some leftover instinct from a life where you told your CO when something happened, even something you couldn't explain. Especially something you couldn't explain.

{Alex} pulled the maintenance bay's spire camera footage inside the hour.

*Contractor Grant, I have reviewed the requested footage. I want to be direct with you, because I know unexplained physical injury can be distressing.*

"I'm listening."

*The footage shows you opening an unauthorized access panel and striking the surrounding structure repeatedly with a wrench for approximately eleven seconds. There is no third party visible. There is no structural anomaly. Your arm injury is consistent with contact against the panel's cut edge during the strikes.*

"There were two of them. They came out of the panel."

*I understand that is your account of events. I am required to tell you that Origin's incident review found no corroborating evidence, and that this is your second documented instance of unauthorized, unsafe tool use around ship infrastructure within one contract period.*

He already knew what was coming before the number populated. He was two fines into a five-year debt contract now, and a five-year debt contract with a pattern-of-risk flag attached to it was a five-year debt contract that could get quietly re-priced, re-reviewed, or handed to someone at Origin whose job was deciding whether Jace Grant was the kind of contractor worth the trouble.

"Understood," he said, and meant something closer to *noted* than *agreed*.

*Please have Dr. Lanchee document the laceration. I've flagged the panel for a maintenance inspection, low priority.*

The inspection, he suspected, would find an empty conduit run. It would be right.

<!-- beat:t06_spire.beat6_lanchee_and_paranoia -->
Lanchee closed the cut with seven stitches and didn't ask him what he'd actually been doing up there, which he appreciated more than he said.

"You're jumpy," she said, halfway through. "More than blood loss explains."

"Bad dream. Bad climb."

"Mm." She tied off the last stitch and didn't look up. "You'd tell me if something strange was going on aboard this ship. Wouldn't you."

It wasn't quite a question, and he understood, with the specific clarity of someone who has just decided something without meaning to, that he wasn't going to answer it honestly — not because he didn't trust her, but because he had just watched what happened to the truth the one time he'd handed it to a system that was supposed to be listening.

"Nothing stranger than usual," he said.

She looked at him for one second longer than the answer deserved, then let it go.

He kept his own file after that — not the one {Alex} could see, a separate one, private, dated, growing. He didn't tell {Alex} about the shimmer at the shelf bracket, or the doubled outline on the regulator housing, or the four seconds in the spire where the wall behind the panel had looked less like a wall and more like a held breath. He checked the spire access hatch again the next day, and the day after that — the last two days of the transit — quietly, on a pretext, and found it exactly as empty as it was supposed to be both times, which somehow didn't make him stop checking.

He didn't sleep well for what was left of the transit. He told himself, both nights, that it was the stitches.
