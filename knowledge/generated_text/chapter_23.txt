Chapter 23. No Vampires

Apollo: Artemis sent a complete packet that answers none of the questions.

Athena: That is worse than no packet.

Apollo: Aura says no data is missing.

Athena: Then maybe somebody decided which meaning we were allowed to get.

The first version of the proposal lasted four minutes in Lanchee's hands.

Sophia had called their device a remote sensing array. Three transferred cameras on Sparky captured incompatible projections of the same subject. A physical MRI or computed-tomography scan supplied a comparison model. Jace's local Aura instance aligned timestamps without correcting disagreements. The method could operate from a nearby room and appeared to show internal structure that conventional instruments missed.

The proposal did not say where the cameras went.

It did not say Jace and Sophia went with them.

Lanchee reached the phrase comparable to an MRI in the next room, but showing substantially more, placed the tablet on her desk, and looked at both of them.

"No."

Sophia leaned forward. "Which provision concerns you?"

"The one where you ask me for patients while concealing the mechanism, the location, and the injuries you have already received."

"The mechanism is unknown," Sophia said.

"The location is not."

Lanchee turned to Jace.

"You said an animal cut you in an experimental environment. You also arrived with temperature sensation so impaired that you could not distinguish ice water from tap water. Now you want volunteers. If I cannot understand the risk, neither can they."

Jace looked at Sophia.

Sophia picked up her father's pen, aligned it with the edge of the tablet, and set it down again.

"Close the door," she said.

Lanchee closed it.

Sophia told her.

Not every theory. Not the Chinese room, the missing ships, or what the creature might be doing to station systems. She told Lanchee every fact that could change a medical decision.

Their bodies crossed completely into an adjacent environment. They brought their clothing, implants, masks, and whatever equipment they could transfer. The environment was cold and could hold trapped carbon dioxide. Physical walls became permeable and transparent. Human bodies did not remain asleep in the Real. Jace and Sophia left it.

They had crossed more than fifty times between them.

Sophia had nearly run out of oxygen after separating from Fortuna's motion. A creature had cut Jace through no physical opening. Jace had twice drawn energy through contact without intending to, first from Sophia and then from Misty. The second accident had damaged an AI stack that no physical diagnostic had predicted was at risk.

Lanchee did not interrupt.

When Sophia finished, the clinic office seemed very small.

"Can you prove any of this without asking me to cross?" Lanchee asked.

"Yes," Jace said.

They moved to an empty examination room. Sophia placed a physical camera on Jace, verified his pulse and body temperature, and set one shoe on a scale. Jace put on his mask, lay down, and crossed.

His weight vanished from the scale.

The room camera recorded his clothing, breathing mask, implant hardware, and body disappearing over several minutes. The process was slow enough to look less like a trick than a medical emergency.

From the Astral, Jace pushed a numbered paper card through the examination table.

It appeared on the physical side.

Lanchee did not touch it until Jace returned.

His weight came back unevenly. Nausea folded him over the edge of the bed.

Lanchee checked him before she checked the card.

"You did this while injured," she said.

"Yes."

"You did it while responsible for a dog."

"Yes."

"And you have been asking why I am cautious with caffeine."

"That part now feels less defensible."

Lanchee read the number on the card.

"The answer is still no."

Sophia's expression tightened.

"To patients," Lanchee said. "For now. You will give me your complete exposure history, medical telemetry, and every physical symptom. You will demonstrate the array on printed objects, rock, equipment, and yourselves. We will write a consent form that says what the method can expose, not what you hope it resembles. Then I will decide whether observation alone presents an acceptable risk."

Jace nodded.

"Doctor," Sophia said, "disclosure beyond this room could end our employment, expose the station to an unknown threat, and place the method under national control before we understand it."

"Medical confidentiality covers your health and this proposed study. It does not cover a station hazard you conceal from me. If I find one, I report it."

"Agreed," Jace said.

Sophia took longer.

"Agreed."

That was how the first proposal ended.



The version Lanchee accepted took three days.

They tested printed blocks, ore samples, raw asteroid, water, a dismantled pump, Sparky, and themselves. They did not aim the array at anyone outside the research team. Lanchee recorded Jace's and Sophia's physical baselines before and after each crossing. She stopped one session when Jace's skin temperature remained normal but his temperature judgment drifted. Sophia protested once, saw Lanchee's face, and did not protest twice.

Nick rebuilt the physical instrument package around disagreement rather than accuracy.

The clinic scanner, two reference cameras, a suit diagnostic puck, and two isolated clocks each kept their own record. Copper wire connected every component to a paper tag bearing a handwritten number. No wireless discovery. No shared timing. No cooperative correction.

It looked less like advanced science than a collection of appliances under investigation for a crime.

Nick inspected it without touching anything.

"The clocks will drift," he said.

"That is the point," Jace said.

"The cameras will drop frames."

"Also the point."

Nick looked through the examination-room window at Lanchee and Sophia revising the volunteer form.

"She knows what the boundary is."

"Yes."

"I still do not."

"No."

Nick's jaw tightened.

"Then I am certifying the physical equipment, not your experiment. If Aura finds a medical risk, Lanchee gets it. If it finds a station risk, Kim gets it."

"Same agreement."

"I am reminding you because you collect dangerous facts faster than obligations."

Nick checked Aura's permissions. The local instance could timestamp Jace's verbal markers, compare them with his implant and suit telemetry, and flag contradictions. It could not transmit raw content, query station systems, identify a patient, or change medical settings.

"Your skull temperature rises when it processes," Nick said. "Yellow line, you stop."

"Lanchee already said that."

"Now two people are correct."

He left before the volunteer arrived.

The accepted consent form described an experimental observation method operating from an adjacent nonphysical environment. It stated that the method might reveal internal structure through clothing and walls, that its biological meaning remained unknown, and that no researcher would touch or alter the volunteer. It also stated that the field of view might exceed the target area. Any image outside the approved region would be deleted, and the session would stop.

The volunteer read that paragraph twice.

"You can see through the wall?" she asked.

"Potentially," Sophia said. "That is why the room beyond it is empty and the corridor is screened."

"Can I see where you go?"

"No."

"Can you see me?"

"Yes, but we do not yet understand what the colors and structures mean. That is the purpose of the comparison."

The woman held up her cast. "And you are not going to fix anything."

"Observation only," Jace said. "If we see something unexpected, we return before doing anything else."

She signed.

Lanchee signed beneath her. Jace and Sophia signed last.



Cerberus stayed with Lanchee on the physical side.

Jace had not found evidence that crossing harmed him. He had also stopped treating an absence of evidence as permission to add the dog to every experiment.

Sophia set out bread and water in the empty examination room. Sparky waited beside the transfer point with three cameras, each aimed along a different projection.

The cold opened around them.

The clinic did not resemble the clinic.

Physical walls flattened into overlapping planes. Cabinets became pale frames with no depth. Metal instruments persisted as edges and interruptions. Wood, paper, and ordered cloth held stronger shapes.

Beyond the examination room, people burned.

Jace had seen human light across Fortuna before, but distance and fear had reduced it to color. Here, within a few meters, every person contained motion too dense for sight.

One patient shone blue-white at the center and green along the limbs. Gold threads moved through both colors. Darker structures formed and dissolved with each breath. The light did not stop at the skin. It folded inward through patterns that seemed larger than the body holding them.

Sophia stared.

"Rainbows in human heads," she whispered.

"That is your scientific description?"

"My scientific description will require several more notebooks."

Through the physical wall behind them, Cerberus glowed beside Lanchee's chair. His structure was simpler and warmer than the humans around him. His attention formed a bright direction pointed at the place where Jace had vanished.

Jace looked down at his own hands. Color moved beneath them in branching streams.

A geometric shape rested behind his eyes.

It was not a face. It had no human color. Fine lines opened and closed in repeating layers, each joined to Jace's glow by hundreds of hair-thin connections.

Sophia stepped closer.

"Your AI," she said.

"You can see it?"

"I can see a structure that was not there before. It is very orderly."

Jace addressed the local instance aloud, although sound did not travel normally in the Astral.

"Aura, mark. New visual structure associated with local processing. Sophia observes it too."

Aura's response formed through his implant rather than the room.

Aura: Mark recorded. I do not have a visual feed corresponding to the reported structure.

The geometric light shifted when the system answered.

Sophia followed its connections with her eyes.

"It uses your structure," she said. "I do not think it is consuming it. The lines brighten when you attend to the system, then settle."

"Unspent capacity."

"Do not name it after one observation."

"Temporary label. Unspent Will."

"You named it Will?"

"It is short."

"It is philosophy pretending to be a unit."

"Then you will enjoy correcting me for years."

The first volunteer entered the treatment room on the physical side.

The construction technician sat where Lanchee directed and rested her healing wrist inside the clinic scanner. Its arm made one slow pass from elbow to fingertips while Sparky recorded the same interval from the Astral.

The cast did not exist there as a solid object. Its ordered fibers formed a pale cage around the arm.

Inside that cage, green light moved.

It gathered at the fracture in slow waves, flowed through the break, and returned along the arm. Each pass left the damaged area a little more ordered.

Sophia stopped arguing about names.

"It follows blood flow," Jace said.

"Not exactly. Some of it crosses against the flow."

"Nerve paths?"

"Not exactly."

"You are using my favorite answer."

They watched from the marked distance. They did not touch the volunteer or move closer. Jace spoke observations while Aura compared his attempted speech, pulse, implant load, three Astral feeds, scanner volume, and independent physical clocks.

The green activity intensified when the technician flexed her fingers. Her physical monitor showed a small rise in heart rate and no other change.

Jace looked past the fracture at the light held within the rest of her body. Some patterns remained bound close. Others gathered and loosened with attention, pain, and breath.

"If that can be directed toward damage," he said, "it might be possible to increase healing."

"Or disrupt it," Sophia said.

"And if some of it is reserve, it may be transferable."

Sophia's pen stopped.

Both of them remembered her collapsing in the physical cabin.

Both remembered Misty's lights going dark.

The discovery changed shape. It was no longer only about seeing. It was about access.

"No taking it," Jace said.

"Agreed."

"Not from a person. Not from an AI. Not to prove it can move. Not because we think they have more than they need."

"Agreed."

"No changing anything inside someone without informed consent and medical supervision."

"Yes."

Sophia wrote a heading across a clean page.

Human Observation Protocol.

Under it, Jace added a shorter line.

We are not going to be vampires.

Sophia read it.

"This is not formal language."

"It is memorable language."

She left it on the page.

A nurse crossed the screened corridor outside the physical room.

The screen's ordered fibers blocked most of Jace's view, but not all of it. A shoulder and the edge of a human glow entered beside the screen before the nurse's physical body reached the doorway. Six frames registered a violet knot near her chest and a tightening of gold threads around it.

Jace looked away.

"Stop," he said.

Aura placed the session marker. Sophia turned Sparky toward the approved chair. They checked the buffer without replaying the content.

"Delete the six frames," Sophia said.

Aura: The frames overlap two approved-subject frames by 0.4 seconds. Delete the full interval?

Sophia looked toward the fracture patient.

"Full interval."

Aura deleted it from both camera buffers and verified the local checksums.

"Walls create consent boundaries in the physical world," Sophia said. "Here they do not."

"Then we create them ourselves."

They ended the session rather than redesigning it around a volunteer.



Warmth, weight, and nausea returned together.

Jace opened his eyes on the examination-room floor. Cerberus pulled against Lanchee's grip until she released him, then licked Jace's face with enough force to interfere with the medical check.

"He stayed," Lanchee said.

"He is a professional."

"He complained for eleven consecutive minutes."

"A vocal professional."

Before they reviewed the fracture data, Jace and Sophia told the volunteer about the six unintended frames and their deletion. They explained that the method had shown colored activity around the fracture, but that they did not know whether it represented healing, response to pain, or something else.

"Was I in danger?" she asked.

"We observed no physical change beyond the heart-rate increase in your normal range," Lanchee said. "That is not the same as proving the method safe."

The technician looked at Sophia.

"Can it see through clothes?"

"Yes," Sophia said. "And through walls."

"That seems like something people should know."

"Yes."

"Are you going to tell them?"

Jace thought of Suzi's cameras, the hidden room on Three, and every private cabin they had learned not to look into.

"We need evidence that another team can test," he said. "Until then, the method stays under these controls. We will tell the medical and station authorities what it can expose before we publish how to reproduce it."

The volunteer looked at Lanchee.

"You believe them?"

"I believe the method exposed more than a physical wall should permit," Lanchee said. "I believe they stopped and deleted the unapproved interval. I have not decided what the green activity means."

That answer appeared to satisfy her more than reassurance would have.

"Keep the anonymous fracture measurements," she said. "If you learn whether the green helps the bone, Doctor Lanchee can tell me."

Sophia recorded the added consent and gave her a copy.

Only after the volunteer left did Lanchee cancel the second planned observation.

"A patient was transferred from the Chinese medical section," she said. "He has been unstable for twelve days. The attending system classifies the trend as recovery, but he continues to weaken."

"Why bring him here now?" Sophia asked.

"The transfer summary contains the same pattern you described: individually valid sensors producing a conclusion the patient's condition does not support."

Jace sat straighter.

"What injury?"

"A wound from shoulder to ribs."

The cafeteria returned to him. A Filipino miner leaning over his noodles. Massive wound. Suit intact. Somehow no pressure loss.

"No suit breach," Jace said.

Lanchee's eyes narrowed. "How do you know?"

"I heard about it during my first week."

"The tissue closed. There is no active bleeding and no infection. His blood volume is stable. He eats, but loses weight. He sleeps, but does not recover. Every model says he should improve."

Sophia opened her notebook.

"Implant load?"

"Intermittent spikes. No process claims them."

"Is he conscious?" Jace asked.

"Not reliably. His medical directive authorizes experimental observation when standard diagnostics fail. It does not authorize intervention."

Lanchee pointed toward isolation room two.

"Can your method observe him without entering that room?"

Jace thought of the human light spread through the clinic.

"Yes."

"Can observation harm him?"

"We have found no effect from looking."

"Better answer. Five minutes. No contact. No intervention. You return and report before you act on anything you see."

Sophia answered first.

"Agreed."

They moved the privacy screen to block the corridor, cleared every room outside the authorized field, and crossed from the empty examination room.

The miner's light was easy to find.

It should have filled isolation room two.

Instead it clung close to his body, thin and gray at the edges.

Something broad and amber lay across his back.

Its flattened body covered the old wound from shoulder to ribs. Tendrils passed through the man's neck and spine. With each pulse, color moved out of him and into the thing.

Sophia caught Jace's arm before he stepped forward.

"We promised to return," she said.

The parasite raised its head.

Its mouth opened like a spiral.
